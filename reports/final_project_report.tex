\title{Counterfactual Credit in Constrained Allocation Reinforcement Learning with Path-Dependent Costs}
\author{\name Housen Zhu\thanks{OpenAI Codex assisted with manuscript revision, mathematical exposition, notation checks and LaTeX preparation.}\\
\addr Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering\\
\addr University of Toronto}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A feasible resource allocation can be assembled from several policy branches, but feasibility does not determine how each branch should receive credit for the resulting outcome. This distinction matters when the allocation map couples branch decisions and rewards and costs depend on earlier allocations. We study counterfactual branch credit for sequential allocation with hard simplex and group constraints and an expected path-cost limit. The architecture uses the established two-group Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition (CAOSD) map, Proximal Policy Optimization and an adaptive multiplier inspired by Reward Constrained Policy Optimization. For each branch, a feasible neutral replacement defines a shadow trajectory with its own adjustment-cost and risk memory. We derive how replacement changes the allocation and separate its immediate effect from differences inherited through the trajectory. These relations clarify what the open-loop credit measures and why it is not generally an unbiased policy-gradient baseline. A portfolio instance provides an exploratory comparison on 20 shared unseen synthetic continuations. Credit-mode rankings vary with the actor distribution; the highest observed mean relative wealth does not establish an improvement over the standalone control. All mapped policies preserve allocation feasibility, while the evaluated risk-penalised policies miss the pooled path-cost target. The study identifies a precise credit-assignment problem and the controlled comparisons needed to test its practical value.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction}

Sequential allocation assigns a limited resource among competing entities while accounting for consequences that unfold over time. A computing system may trade immediate throughput against migration costs; an investor may trade expected growth against transaction costs and drawdown. In both cases, an allocation must be admissible when executed, while its value depends on the sequence of allocations and outcomes. Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a way to learn these intertemporal trade-offs.

Admissibility and trajectory performance impose different requirements. Non-negativity, resource conservation and group limits restrict the current action. A cumulative risk limit restricts the outcomes of a policy over time. Enforcing one does not enforce the other: a portfolio can respect every exposure limit and still experience an unacceptable drawdown. Conversely, an acceptable average cost does not by itself exclude an inadmissible allocation. This distinction motivates a feasible action construction coupled to adaptive learning of path costs.

A further question arises when the construction decomposes an allocation into several learned branches: \emph{which branch should receive credit for the realised outcome?} A branch evaluated in isolation need not have the same value inside the combined allocation. Its mass may be small, its choice may change the masses assigned to other branches, and the final adjustment cost depends on the combined action. With path-dependent costs, two otherwise identical allocations can also produce different losses because their preceding allocations and wealth histories differ. Credit therefore depends on both the allocation map and the trajectory state.

This paper studies that problem within a concrete, implemented architecture. Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition (CAOSD) supplies feasible actions under two possibly overlapping group limits \citep{winkel2023}. An autoregressive policy selects the branch variables, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates their distributions \citep{schulman2017}. A multiplier inspired by Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) adjusts the penalty on realised path cost \citep{tessler2019}. Counterfactual (CF) credit compares the executed allocation with a feasible allocation obtained by replacing one branch with a fixed neutral choice. Each replacement path maintains its own history. The central question is how to define and interpret this comparison when branch choices and path statistics are dependent.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}

Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) express reward maximisation subject to expected cost limits \citep{kushwaha2025}. Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) develops policy updates using bounds on reward and cost changes \citep{achiam2017}; RCPO uses a learned penalty to guide policy improvement \citep{tessler2019}. These approaches address expected outcomes. Action-constrained RL instead restricts each action to an admissible set \citep{lin2021}. For allocations, CAOSD constructs feasible actions through coupled simplex-valued branches, while Polytope Action Space Policy Optimization (PASPO) addresses more general linear constraints \citep{winkel2023,winkel2024paspo}. We use the two-group CAOSD map and retain that restriction throughout.

Credit assignment is a separate line of work. Counterfactual multi-agent policy gradients use an action-marginalised baseline to assess individual decisions \citep{foerster2018}. Difference rewards compare outcomes after replacing one component and can exploit a known reward model \citep{castellini2025}. Our setting differs in two relevant respects: the components are conditional branches of one policy, and the replacement is propagated through a feasible map and a persistent auxiliary trajectory. The resulting signal is a fixed-replacement comparison, whose interpretation must be established directly rather than inherited from a multi-agent baseline theorem.

Portfolio allocation makes these dependencies explicit through transaction costs and wealth-based risk \citep{jiang2017,gollart2025}. More broadly, combining a known structural model with sequential learning can reduce the decisions left to the policy, as illustrated in resource-control applications such as \citet{ding2021}. Here the structural model establishes allocation feasibility; it does not solve the trajectory optimisation problem.

\subsection{Scope and contributions}

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item It specifies a constrained allocation problem that separates hard action support from expected path cost, including the allocation and risk memory required by the environment and the information actually supplied to the policy.
  \item It defines feasible neutral-replacement credit for dependent allocation branches and derives two identities that expose map coupling and inherited path effects. These give a precise interpretation of the implemented open-loop comparison.
  \item It documents the associated branch objectives and adaptive penalty update, and compares the implemented credit schemes in a portfolio instance with shared evaluation paths.
\end{enumerate}
CAOSD, PPO, RCPO and difference-based credit retain their original attribution. The research focus is their interaction in a stateful allocation problem. The current experiments provide exploratory evidence about complete training schemes; isolating the contribution of credit requires the controlled comparisons discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The model is stated for general allocation, whereas the empirical evidence is specific to the portfolio environment.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

Sequential allocation concerns a resource that must be divided among several entities as conditions change. The decision is not simply which allocation performs best at the present moment. Moving away from an earlier allocation can incur a cost, and the outcomes accumulated so far can change how the next decision should be assessed. A policy must therefore account for both the allocation it chooses now and the history from which that choice is made. We distinguish requirements that every allocation must satisfy from limits on costs accumulated over a trajectory.

The portfolio environment gives these ideas a concrete meaning. Resource shares become investment weights, changing those weights incurs a transaction-cost proxy, and the resulting wealth history determines drawdown. Market outcomes are external to the investor, but transaction costs and risk depend on the investor's decisions. We first describe the general decision process and its constrained objective, then specify the portfolio dynamics and risk accounting. This provides a common model for evaluating the executed allocation and the auxiliary replacement paths used later for branch credit.

\subsection{Sequential Allocation and Information Structure}

Consider a divisible resource distributed among $N$ entities over $H$ decision periods. The entity set is $\mathcal E=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$, and decisions are indexed by $t=0,\ldots,H-1$. An action $\bm a_t$ specifies the resource share assigned to each entity. At the start of a period, the policy observes the information available at that time and selects an allocation. The environment then produces an outcome, after which the reward, costs and internal state are updated. Decisions cannot use outcomes that have not yet occurred.

We describe the complete environment by a finite-horizon constrained Markov decision process (CMDP),
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,R,\gamma,\mathcal C).
  \label{eq:allocation-mdp}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal S$ is the state space and $\mathcal A$ is the allocation space. The transition kernel $P$ specifies the distribution of the next state conditional on the current state and action. The reward function $R$ assigns a scalar reward to that transition, and $\gamma\in(0,1]$ determines the weight placed on future rewards. The collection $\mathcal C$ contains $Q$ transition-cost functions, denoted by $C_q$ for $q=1,\ldots,Q$. The horizon $H$ and initial-state distribution $\mu_0$ complete the specification. Reward and cost returns are assumed integrable under the policies considered.

Reallocation costs and path-dependent risk require information beyond current external conditions. The same target allocation can incur different reallocation costs depending on the previous allocation, even when external conditions are unchanged. The state must therefore retain the previous allocation to determine the cost of reallocation. Similarly, risk measures defined over a trajectory require sufficient statistics of past outcomes. Together, these variables provide the memory needed to calculate rewards and costs as new outcomes are realised.

We therefore write the complete state as
\begin{equation}
  s_t=(x_t,\bm a_{t-1},\zeta_t,t).
  \label{eq:augmented-state}
\end{equation}
The component $x_t$ contains the external variables and history sufficient to describe their evolution. The previous target is $\bm a_{t-1}$, while $\zeta_t$ collects the remaining internal statistics needed to update outcomes and path risk. Time is included because the remaining decision horizon can affect the value of an action. We assume this complete state is Markov: once $s_t$ and $\bm a_t$ are given, earlier history provides no further information about the next-state distribution,
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_t,\bm a_t).
  \label{eq:allocation-transition}
\end{equation}
State augmentation is useful here because it replaces the relevant parts of a potentially long history with quantities that can be updated recursively. The portfolio instance below specifies those quantities explicitly.

The complete state used to define the environment need not be fully visible to the policy. Let $\mathsf O$ be the observation map, so that the policy receives
\begin{equation}
  o_t=\mathsf O(s_t).
  \label{eq:allocation-observation}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal F_t$ denote all information revealed before the decision, including observations, earlier actions and realised rewards and costs. A general non-anticipative policy $\pi_t(\cdot\mid\mathcal F_t)$ may use this available history, but cannot use future outcomes. In our implementation, the feedforward actor selects actions from the current observation \(o_t\), which combines a finite window of market returns with allocation and risk statistics. These statistics retain the information needed to calculate reallocation costs and update path risk. They do not reveal the latent market regime, so the actor operates under partial observation.

\subsection{Feasible Actions and Constrained Objective}

The first requirement is resource conservation. Each action must allocate the whole resource without assigning a negative share:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A=\AllocSimplex
    :=\left\{\bm a\in\R^N:
       a_j\geq0\ \text{for all }j\in\mathcal E,\
       \mathbf1^\top\bm a=1\right\}.
  \label{eq:allocation-simplex}
\end{equation}
The superscript $N$ counts allocation coordinates, and $\mathbf1$ is the vector of ones. Additional requirements can reserve a minimum share for a group of entities. For two prescribed groups $V_1,V_2\subseteq\mathcal E$ and minimum shares $\ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1]$, the hard-feasible set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\mathrm h}
    =\left\{\bm a\in\AllocSimplex:
       \sum_{j\in V_k}a_j\geq\ell_k,\quad k=1,2\right\}.
  \label{eq:hard-action-set}
\end{equation}
The groups may overlap, in which case a share assigned to their intersection contributes towards both requirements. We assume the limits are mutually feasible. A maximum share $u$ for one group can also be expressed as a minimum share $1-u$ for its complement, because all shares sum to one. The implemented construction handles two group inequalities of this form.

These requirements apply when an action is executed. Define $\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ as the class of non-anticipative policies whose action distributions satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t(\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}\mid\mathcal F_t)=1
  \quad\text{almost surely for every }t=0,\ldots,H-1.
  \label{eq:hard-feasibility}
\end{equation}
This condition makes the distinction between feasible sampling and penalised learning precise. Softmax and Dirichlet constructions can produce simplex-valued samples, but they do not by themselves enforce the additional group limits. A finite violation penalty changes the desirability of an action without restricting its support. Exactly zero expected non-negative violation would imply zero violation almost surely; a small estimated penalty during learning does not establish that condition. We therefore impose allocation feasibility structurally and use the cumulative-cost objective for consequences that depend on a trajectory.

For a policy $\pi$, expected discounted reward is
\begin{equation}
  J_R(\pi)
    =\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^t
       R(s_t,\bm a_t,s_{t+1})\right].
  \label{eq:reward-return}
\end{equation}
The expectation includes initial-state, transition and policy randomness. For each cost function, define the expected episode-average cost as
\begin{equation}
  J_{C_q}(\pi)
    =\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}C_q(s_t,\bm a_t,s_{t+1})\right].
  \label{eq:cost-return}
\end{equation}
Averaging gives every period the same weight in the cost requirement; the reward objective separately uses $\gamma$ to discount future utility. Given a fixed limit $d_q$ for each of the $Q$ cost functions, the allocation problem is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}}\quad &J_R(\pi)\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
      &J_{C_q}(\pi)\leq d_q.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hybrid-problem}
\end{equation}

The two parts of this problem constrain different objects. Membership in $\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ makes every selected allocation admissible. The cost inequalities limit the policy's expected cumulative consequences, allowing costs to vary across periods and trajectories. This constraint limits the expected average cost over the horizon. Individual periods and realised trajectories may still exceed the corresponding cost target. The following portfolio instance specifies how a history-dependent financial risk can enter this formulation as a transition cost.

\subsection{Portfolio Instantiation}

In the portfolio instance, the resource is investment capital. The action is written as $\bm w_t=\bm a_t$, where $w_{t,j}$ is the target fraction of capital allocated to asset $j$. Coordinate $0$ represents cash, which earns zero return in the model. The investor chooses $\bm w_t$ at time $t$, before observing the returns over the following period. The vector of simple asset returns is
\begin{equation}
  \bm R_{t+1}
    =\bigl(0,R_{t+1,1},\ldots,R_{t+1,N-1}\bigr)^\top
    \in\R^N.
  \label{eq:asset-return-vector}
\end{equation}
For a non-cash asset with price $p_{t,j}>0$ at time $t$, its simple return is the fractional price change,
\begin{equation}
  R_{t+1,j}
    =\frac{p_{t+1,j}-p_{t,j}}{p_{t,j}},
  \qquad j=1,\ldots,N-1.
  \label{eq:asset-simple-return}
\end{equation}
The cash entry is zero. These expressions specify the financial meaning of the return vector; the synthetic environment generates returns directly rather than computing them from observed prices. Market returns are assumed to be unaffected by the investor's allocation.

Changing the target incurs a fee proportional to turnover. The simulator measures that turnover by the full-vector change from the preceding target,
\begin{equation}
  T_t=\lVert\bm w_t-\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1.
  \label{eq:portfolio-turnover}
\end{equation}
The norm sums absolute weight changes, including transfers to and from the reserve. With fee coefficient $\kappa\geq0$, the resulting net growth factor is
\begin{equation}
  g_{t+1}=1+\bm w_t^\top\bm R_{t+1}-\kappa T_t.
  \label{eq:portfolio-net-growth}
\end{equation}
Thus the same market return can produce different net outcomes when reached through different reallocations. Starting from normalised wealth $W_0=1$, the account evolves according to
\begin{equation}
  W_{t+1}=W_t\max\{\varepsilon_W,g_{t+1}\}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-wealth}
\end{equation}
The transition reward is the logarithm of the numerically protected growth factor,
\begin{equation}
  r_t=R(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})
     =\log\!\bigl(\max\{\varepsilon_R,g_{t+1}\}\bigr).
  \label{eq:portfolio-reward}
\end{equation}
The positive constants $\varepsilon_W$ and $\varepsilon_R$ are numerical floors. When both are inactive, the reward equals the change in log wealth. At the first decision, the previous target $\bm w_{-1}$ is the constrained-neutral allocation. Previous-turnover memory $T_{-1}$ is initialised to zero.

This accounting uses changes in target weights as a transaction-cost proxy. Exact holdings-level self-financing would instead measure trades from return-drifted holdings and account for fee payment when determining post-trade capital and weights \citep{jiang2017}. The simulator omits slippage, tiered fees and market impact, and does not charge the constant-target benchmark for rebalancing caused by holding drift. Appendix~\ref{app:instance} gives the numerical constants, initial allocations and a sufficient condition for positive net growth. These conventions determine the financial interpretation of both rewards and risk.

The risk cost asks how far wealth has fallen below a previously attained level. The running wealth peak is
\begin{equation}
  M_t=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}W_v.
  \label{eq:portfolio-peak}
\end{equation}
Here $v$ indexes elapsed periods, including the initial account value. Current drawdown is the fractional shortfall from that peak,
\begin{equation}
  D_t=1-\frac{W_t}{M_t}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-drawdown}
\end{equation}
Maximum drawdown records the largest such shortfall observed so far,
\begin{equation}
  D_t^{\max}=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}D_v.
  \label{eq:portfolio-max-drawdown}
\end{equation}
A positive return can reduce current drawdown without eliminating it, because recovering part of a previous loss need not restore the earlier wealth peak. This is why the next return alone cannot determine the risk cost. The account's wealth and peak must also be retained. Current drawdown determines the training cost below; maximum drawdown remains an observation and evaluation statistic.

A constrained-neutral benchmark follows a fixed feasible target on the same market returns. Superscript $B$ denotes its wealth, peak and drawdown statistics, and its initial wealth and peak are both one. The benchmark provides a reference for how severe losses are on the shared market path. The investor's drawdown is still measured relative to its own peak, but the threshold against which it is assessed adapts to the benchmark:
\begin{equation}
  b_{t+1}=\max\{b_{\min},\beta D_{t+1}^B\}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-risk-threshold}
\end{equation}
The parameter $b_{\min}>0$ supplies a positive threshold floor, while $\beta>0$ controls the response to benchmark drawdown. The cost charges only the amount by which current drawdown exceeds that threshold,
\begin{equation}
  c_{t+1}=\frac{[D_{t+1}-b_{t+1}]_+^2}{s_C}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-cost}
\end{equation}
The notation $[x]_+$ means $\max(0,x)$, and $s_C>0$ scales the squared excess. Squaring makes deeper exceedances more costly, while repeated exceedances accumulate through the episode-average objective. A cost allowance on the same scale is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{t+1}=\frac{(\nu b_{t+1})^2}{s_C}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-alpha}
\end{equation}
The non-negative parameter $\nu$ sets this allowance relative to the moving threshold. The two quantities have separate roles: $b_{t+1}$ is a drawdown level, whereas $\alpha_{t+1}$ specifies the cost level used in the average constraint.

All of these updates follow the decision timing introduced above. At decision $t$, the policy has access to the current threshold $b_t$. Only after the next market return is realised are both accounts updated and $b_{t+1}$, $c_{t+1}$ and $\alpha_{t+1}$ computed. Their dependence on the next return makes them outcomes of the transition, not information supplied in advance to the policy.

The internal memory needed by this account model and its diagnostics is
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_t=(W_t,M_t,D_t^{\max},
           W_t^B,M_t^B,D_t^{B,\max},T_{t-1}).
  \label{eq:portfolio-path-state}
\end{equation}
Wealth and peaks recover current drawdowns, while the other entries retain maximum drawdowns and previous turnover for the observation. Together with the previous allocation and external state, these quantities support recursive updates without replaying the complete trading history.

The actual observation provides market information through trailing returns and their rolling means and volatility. It describes the investor's position through previous weights, turnover and group-exposure diagnostics, and reports the current risk quantities, threshold gap and elapsed fraction of the horizon. A turnover-slack diagnostic is also included; it does not introduce another hard action constraint. Absolute wealth and latent market regimes are not actor inputs. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} details these components and their sources.

Finally, the portfolio risk requirement is obtained from the general constrained objective by using one centred transition cost,
\begin{equation}
  C_1(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})=c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-centred-cost}
\end{equation}
Its fixed limit is $d_1=0$, giving
\begin{equation}
  J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi)
    :=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}
       (c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1})\right]\leq0.
  \label{eq:adaptive-risk-problem}
\end{equation}
Keeping the random allowance inside the expectation makes clear that the requirement concerns average residual cost under a benchmark-dependent tolerance. The hard allocation limits remain unchanged throughout the trajectory. Maximum drawdown and terminal relative wealth, measured by $W_H/W_H^B-1$, provide additional portfolio evaluation statistics. Section~\ref{sec:learning} describes how the learner approximates this constrained objective using branch advantages and an adaptive multiplier.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\label{sec:reformulation}

This section introduces the feasible map and policy-learning tools used below. Their established properties are separated from the branch-credit choices studied in this paper.

\subsection{Two-group simplex decomposition}

The learner samples a surrogate action $\bm u_t$ and converts it to an allocation through a deterministic map,
\begin{equation}
  \bm a_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}.
  \label{eq:surrogate-map}
\end{equation}
The environment uses the mapped allocation to calculate its transition, reward and cost. In the CAOSD construction \citep{winkel2023}, branch indices $i=1,\ldots,4$ refer to four prescribed supports:
\begin{equation}
  K_1=V_1\cap V_2,\qquad K_2=V_1,\qquad
  K_3=V_2,\qquad K_4=\mathcal E.
  \label{eq:caosd-branches}
\end{equation}
The component $\bm u_i$ determines $\bm y_i\in\R_+^N$, a probability vector on $K_i$, padded with zeros outside that support. For an empty support, set $\bm y_i=\bm0$. The overlap share of branch~2 is
\begin{equation}
  q_2=\sum_{j\in V_1\cap V_2}y_{2,j}.
  \label{eq:q2}
\end{equation}
The map mixes these branch allocations with coefficients
\begin{align}
  z_1&=[\ell_1+\ell_2-1]_+,&
  z_2&=[\ell_1-z_1]_+,\nonumber\\
  z_3&=[\ell_2-z_1-z_2q_2]_+,&
  z_4&=1-z_1-z_2-z_3,
  \label{eq:caosd-masses}\\
  \mathsf T(\bm u)
    &=\CAOSD(\bm y_1,\ldots,\bm y_4)
      =\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i\bm y_i.
  \label{eq:caosd}
\end{align}
Branch~1 supplies mandatory overlap mass, branch~2 completes the first-group requirement, branch~3 supplies any remaining second-group requirement, and branch~4 allocates the residual. In particular, $q_2$ makes the later masses depend on branch~2. A time subscript denotes the coefficient obtained from the branches sampled at decision $t$.

\begin{proposition}[Two-group CAOSD feasibility; \citealp{winkel2023}]
\label{prop:feasibility}
Suppose $\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}$ is non-empty and every non-empty branch is a probability vector on its prescribed support. Then $z_i\geq0$, $\sum_i z_i=1$, and every empty branch has zero coefficient. The allocation $\mathsf T(\bm u)$ satisfies the simplex and both group limits in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:allocation-simplex}--\eqref{eq:hard-action-set}.
\end{proposition}
The proof in Appendix~\ref{app:feasibility} includes disjoint groups and zero-mass branches. Feasibility is an algebraic property of the map, independent of critic accuracy and policy convergence. Floating-point checks use a numerical tolerance. The implemented neural actor additionally requires each $K_i$ to be non-empty; this is satisfied by the experimental groups.

\subsection{Adaptive penalties and policy improvement}

For one expected-cost constraint, let $J_C$ denote its cost return and $d_C$ its limit. The Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)
    =J_R(\pi)-\lambda\bigl(J_C(\pi)-d_C\bigr),
  \qquad \lambda\geq0.
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
The multiplier $\lambda$ sets the pressure on cost. A positive estimated constraint residual increases that pressure. RCPO couples policy learning to a slower penalty update and permits a discounted guiding penalty \citep{tessler2019}. We use that design principle; the implemented schedule and branch objectives are given in Section~\ref{sec:learning}.

PPO updates a stochastic policy from stored decisions by comparing their probabilities under the current and rollout policies \citep{schulman2017}. For probability-density ratio $\rho$, advantage estimate $A$, and clipping width $0<\epsilon<1$, define
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_\epsilon(\rho,A)
    =\min\{\rho A,\operatorname{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-surrogate}
\end{equation}
An advantage estimates how favourable an outcome is relative to the critic's prediction of future accumulated feedback. The operator $\operatorname{clip}$ truncates its first argument to the displayed interval. Clipping limits the incentive to increase or decrease the probability of a stored action within this surrogate objective.

The same advantage estimator is used for each selected scalar signal $f_t$, whether a reward, cost or difference. Let $v_t^f$ be its stored critic prediction and $m_t$ equal zero at an episode-ending transition and one otherwise. Generalised advantage estimation (GAE) \citep{schulman2015gae} uses the backward recursion
\begin{equation}
  \delta_t^f=f_t+\gamma m_t v_{t+1}^f-v_t^f,\qquad
  \widehat A_t^f=\delta_t^f+
      \gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}m_t\widehat A_{t+1}^f.
  \label{eq:gae}
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_t^f$ is a one-step prediction error and $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}\in[0,1]$ controls how many future errors contribute. At the end of a stored rollout, the next advantage is set to zero, while the next value is bootstrapped if the episode continues. Critics regress to targets $v_t^f+\widehat A_t^f$. Below, $A$ always denotes such an estimate; the hat is omitted to keep branch notation readable. The multiplier $\lambda$ and GAE parameter $\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}$ serve different purposes.

\subsection{Credit for dependent branches}

A shared portfolio outcome, the performance of an isolated branch, and a neutral-replacement difference answer different questions. A shared signal evaluates the complete allocation. A standalone signal evaluates a branch as if it received the whole resource. A replacement difference compares two combined allocations under a specified intervention.

The baseline argument in counterfactual policy-gradient methods depends on the sampling structure \citep{foerster2018,castellini2025}. For an autoregressive policy, later branch values depend on earlier ones. Holding those later values fixed after replacing an earlier branch is therefore a specific open-loop comparison. It does not generally produce an action-independent baseline for the earlier branch. Persistent path state introduces a further distinction between changing the current action and following a sequence of earlier replacements. The next section defines the implemented comparison and makes both dependencies explicit.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:learning}

The architecture has three interfaces. The actor samples branch variables, CAOSD constructs an executable allocation, and the realised transition supplies learning signals. Counterfactual paths alter the branch signals; the global risk multiplier always responds to actual portfolio cost.

\subsection{Feasible autoregressive policy}

Let $\theta$ denote the actor parameters and $\pi_\theta^u$ the distribution over surrogate actions. Its allocation distribution is $\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}=\mathsf T_\#\pi_\theta^u$, where the pushforward symbol means that an allocation set receives the probability of all surrogate samples mapped into it. Learning is restricted to this family:
\begin{equation}
  \max_\theta J_R(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})\leq0,
  \qquad \pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}.
  \label{eq:restricted-problem}
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility} supplies the hard condition. The neural policy and optimiser determine which feasible behaviours are learned; this parameterisation is not claimed to cover every admissible policy.

Let $\mathcal I\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}$ be the fixed set of trainable branches and let $\bm y_{<i,t}=(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_{i-1,t})$ collect the preceding realised branches. The actor factorises as
\begin{equation}
  \pi_\theta^u(\bm u_t\mid o_t)
    =\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}
      \pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t}).
  \label{eq:autoregressive-policy}
\end{equation}
A shared multilayer perceptron encodes the observation. Successive distribution heads receive that representation and the earlier branch weights. A Gaussian head samples $|K_i|$ real-valued scores, or logits; softmax exponentiates each score and divides by their sum to obtain branch weights. A Dirichlet head samples the weights directly, using bounded positive concentration parameters to control their distribution on the simplex. Both produce a probability vector on the prescribed support. Likelihoods refer to the sampled logits or weights, respectively, rather than to the many-to-one mapped allocation.

Masked branches use deterministic neutral values and contribute no likelihood term. The reported instance has $\mathcal I=\{2,3,4\}$ because $z_1=0$ identically. A temporarily vanishing mass in another branch does not change this fixed training mask.

Let $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ denote the parameters used to collect the rollout. The conditional and joint density ratios are
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{i,t}(\theta)=
    \frac{\pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}},i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})},
  \qquad
  \rho_t(\theta)=\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}\rho_{i,t}(\theta).
  \label{eq:ppo-ratios}
\end{equation}
Numerator and denominator use the same stored predecessor values. Joint PPO uses $\rho_t$; the branch objectives below clip each $\rho_{i,t}$ separately. These are different update rules even when they receive the same scalar outcome.

\subsection{Standalone and feasible-replacement credit}

The standalone reference invests the entire resource according to $\bm y_{i,t}$. Each such auxiliary account has its own previous weights and wealth history, producing a standalone reward $r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}$. This evaluates isolated branch performance, rather than its contribution to the mapped portfolio. The reference used in the experiments combines this reward credit with the \emph{actual portfolio's} cost credit. Auxiliary standalone allocations are never executed and need not satisfy both exposure limits.

For counterfactual credit, let $\bm y_i^0$ be uniform on $K_i$ and zero elsewhere; an empty support has the zero vector. Replace branch $i$ and recompute the complete feasible map:
\begin{align}
  \bm a_t^{\mathrm{act}}
    &=\CAOSD(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_{4,t}),\nonumber\\
  \bm a_t^{-i}
    &=\CAOSD(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_i^0,\ldots,\bm y_{4,t}).
  \label{eq:counterfactual}
\end{align}
The superscript $-i$ denotes a neutral replacement, not removal of resource. All other branch values remain as sampled in the actual decision. Both allocations satisfy the same hard limits by Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility}. Uniform replacement is an explicit reference choice, rather than an optimal or policy-averaged alternative.

A separate shadow account for branch $i$ follows the sequence $\bm a_0^{-i},\bm a_1^{-i},\ldots$ on the same market returns. It starts from the actual account's neutral target and normalised wealth one, then updates its own previous target, turnover, wealth, peak and drawdown using the accounting equations of Section~\ref{sec:model}. Standalone accounts instead start from their respective uniform branch allocations, also at wealth one. The shadow reward $r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}}$ and cost $c_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{cf}}$ give the signed differences
\begin{equation}
  \Delta r_{i,t}=r_t-r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}},\qquad
  \Delta c_{i,t+1}=c_{t+1}-c_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{cf}}.
  \label{eq:counterfactual-signals}
\end{equation}
A positive reward difference favours the actual path; a positive cost difference means it incurs more cost than the replacement path. The benchmark and allowance are shared, so subtracting centred costs gives the same $\Delta c$. Shadow memories persist across rollouts and reset with the episode. Critics for difference signals receive the actual observation and the corresponding shadow-path features: weights, previous turnover, wealth relative to the actual account, drawdowns, threshold gap and elapsed time.

This comparison is open-loop in two senses. Later branches are not resampled after an earlier replacement, and future actions come from the actual actor rather than from a policy observing the shadow account. The construction uses known portfolio accounting and exogenous returns. In an allocation system whose external dynamics depend on the action, the same comparison would require a valid transition model or simulator.

The branches can be viewed as virtual decision makers sharing one resource, but they remain modules of one autoregressive policy. They share parameters, act within the same environment decision and do not define a separate decentralised multi-agent algorithm.

\subsection{What the replacement difference measures}
\label{sec:credit-analysis}

\paragraph{Dependence through the feasible map.}
Replacing branch~2 can affect the allocation of mass to branches~3 and~4. Let $z_3^{-2}$ be the coefficient recomputed after replacing $\bm y_2$ by $\bm y_2^0$. With the other branch vectors fixed, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:caosd-masses}--\eqref{eq:caosd} give the exact identity
\begin{equation}
  \bm a^{\mathrm{act}}-\bm a^{-2}
    =z_2(\bm y_2-\bm y_2^0)
      +(z_3-z_3^{-2})(\bm y_3-\bm y_4).
  \label{eq:coupling-identity}
\end{equation}
The first term changes branch~2's own allocation. The second redistributes mass between later branches because the overlap share changed. It vanishes when the replacement leaves $z_3$ unchanged, including when both versions already require no additional third-branch mass. Thus branch size alone does not determine its effect. Re-evaluating the complete map captures this redistribution; replacing only $z_2\bm y_2$ does not.

\paragraph{Dependence through path memory.}
Let $h_t=(\bm a_{t-1},\zeta_t)$ be actual internal memory and $h_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}}$ the corresponding shadow memory. Conditional on the same realised external outcome, write $F_t^f(h,\bm a)$ for the signal produced by applying action $\bm a$ from memory $h$, where $f=r$ selects reward and $f=c$ selects transition cost. Then
\begin{align}
  &F_t^f(h_t,\bm a_t^{\mathrm{act}})
     -F_t^f(h_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}},\bm a_t^{-i})\nonumber\\
  &\quad=
    \underbrace{F_t^f(h_t,\bm a_t^{\mathrm{act}})
       -F_t^f(h_t,\bm a_t^{-i})}_{\text{current-action contrast}}
    +\underbrace{F_t^f(h_t,\bm a_t^{-i})
       -F_t^f(h_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}},\bm a_t^{-i})}_{\text{inherited-history contrast}}.
  \label{eq:history-identity}
\end{align}
This is an accounting identity obtained by adding and subtracting the same term. The first contrast holds prior memory fixed. The second measures the consequence of differing prior memories under the same replacement action. The implementation uses their sum; it does not separately estimate the two terms. Equal current allocations can therefore coexist with non-zero credit when previous weights or drawdown histories differ.

\paragraph{Implication for learning.}
The difference is an interpretable comparison with a specified replacement history. It is not generally an unbiased causal effect of the current branch or a gradient-preserving baseline. In particular, a baseline that retains later autoregressive samples can remain statistically dependent on the replaced branch. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline} gives a two-variable example. This distinction motivates treating counterfactual credit as a learning signal to be tested, with the neutral choice, retained downstream values and path-memory convention stated explicitly.

\subsection{Risk-penalised branch objectives}

For the portfolio constraint, the Lagrangian relaxation is
$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ad}}(\theta,\lambda)
=J_R(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})-\lambda J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})$.
Let $A_t^R$ and $A_t^C$ denote GAE estimates from actual reward $r_t$ and actual cost $c_{t+1}$. The cost critic omits $\alpha_{t+1}$ because its expectation is policy-independent under the fixed-horizon exogenous-market protocol; Appendix~\ref{app:instance} establishes this property. It does not make discounted GAE an exact gradient of the undiscounted constraint.

Let $A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}$ be the estimate from $r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}$, and let $A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}$ and $A_{i,t}^{\Delta C}$ be estimates from the differences in Eq.~\eqref{eq:counterfactual-signals}. The combined signals are
\begin{align}
  A_t^{\mathrm{global}}&=A_t^R-\lambda A_t^C,\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}&=A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}-\lambda A_t^C,\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-R}}&=A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}-\lambda A_t^C,\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-C}}&=z_{i,t}A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}-\lambda A_{i,t}^{\Delta C},\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-RC}}&=A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}-\lambda A_{i,t}^{\Delta C}.
  \label{eq:branch-advantages}
\end{align}
The suffixes R, C and RC indicate reward replacement, cost replacement and both. The standalone reference shares the global cost estimate across branches. In CF-C, only the standalone reward estimate is multiplied by its allocation mass before combination.

A tilde denotes normalisation of a combined estimate using its rollout mean and standard deviation, separately for each branch. With $\widehat{\mathbb E}_t$ denoting the empirical mean over stored transitions, the objectives are
\begin{align}
  L_{\mathrm{global}}(\theta)
    &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t[
        \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_t,\widetilde A_t^{\mathrm{global}})],
    \label{eq:rcpo-advantage}\\
  L_{\mathrm{st}}(\theta)
    &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
        \sum_{i\in\mathcal I}z_{i,t}
        \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_{i,t},\widetilde A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}})\right],
    \label{eq:standalone-surrogate}\\
  L_{\mathrm{CF},m}(\theta)
    &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
        \sum_{i\in\mathcal I}
        \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_{i,t},\widetilde A_{i,t}^{m})\right],
    \quad m\in\{\mathrm{CF\!-\!R},\mathrm{CF\!-\!C},\mathrm{CF\!-\!RC}\}.
    \label{eq:counterfactual-surrogate}
\end{align}
Recorded masses and the fixed training mask are held constant during each update. Standalone mass weights multiply the entire clipped surrogate, including its cost term. Counterfactual differences already describe the combined portfolio, so the implemented counterfactual objective applies no further mass factor. In CF-C, the reward mass factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:branch-advantages} appears once, before normalisation. None of these branch surrogates is asserted to be an algebraic decomposition of the global objective.

Entropy regularisation encourages exploration of the surrogate variables. Let $e_{i,t}(\theta)$ be the conditional differential entropy of branch $i$ in its sampled coordinates. The actor maximises $L_m+\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\mathcal H_m$, where $\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\geq0$ is the entropy coefficient and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H_m(\theta)
    =\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
        \sum_{i\in\mathcal I}\omega_{i,t}^{m}e_{i,t}(\theta)\right],
  \qquad
  \omega_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}=z_{i,t},\quad
  \omega_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF}}=1.
  \label{eq:branch-entropy}
\end{equation}
Differential entropy is defined for a continuous distribution and can be negative. Equal coefficients do not imply equal regularisation across modes or sampling coordinates. Credit signals, external weights, entropy weights and critic inputs all differ in the archived configurations, so their comparison concerns complete training schemes.

\subsection{Adaptive multiplier and training procedure}

The multiplier is fixed during collection and all PPO epochs of a rollout. After that actor--critic update, it changes once:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\leftarrow[\lambda+\eta_\lambda(\bar c-\bar\alpha)]_+,\qquad
  \eta_\lambda=
  \begin{cases}
    \eta_\uparrow,&\bar c>\bar\alpha,\\
    \eta_\downarrow,&\bar c\leq\bar\alpha.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
Here $\bar c$ and $\bar\alpha$ are actual rollout averages; $\eta_\uparrow$ and $\eta_\downarrow$ are the respective step sizes for increasing and decreasing the multiplier. A positive residual raises the penalty, while a negative residual relaxes it subject to non-negativity. All credit modes use actual non-negative cost in this update, including those with signed branch-cost differences. PPO controls set $\lambda=0$ and omit the update.

This is an RCPO-inspired adaptive penalty procedure. Discounted guiding advantages, undiscounted dual statistics, normalisation and branchwise clipping separate the implemented updates from exact optimisation of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ad}}$. The original RCPO convergence analysis does not establish constraint satisfaction for these modified updates. Hard allocation feasibility still holds because it comes from $\mathsf T$, rather than from the risk optimiser.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:training} summarises the implemented sequence. Ongoing account memories cross rollout boundaries; they reset only when an episode ends. During training, the replacement construction requires one shadow account per active branch, plus any standalone accounts needed by the selected mode. With $|\mathcal I|$ active branches and $N$ entities, their target vectors require $O(|\mathcal I|N)$ storage and their accounting requires $O(|\mathcal I|N)$ work per market step, excluding neural-network computation. This arithmetic estimate does not measure the additional critic or optimisation cost. Deployment uses the actor and feasible map without shadow accounts.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Feasible allocation learning with branch credit}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Credit mode $m$, initial actor and critics, non-negative $\lambda$.
\State Initialise actual, benchmark and required auxiliary accounts.
\While{the prescribed training budget remains}
  \State Fix rollout actor $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\gets\theta$ and multiplier $\lambda$.
  \For{each transition in the rollout}
    \State Observe $o_t$; sample $\bm u_t$ autoregressively using Eq.~\eqref{eq:autoregressive-policy}.
    \State Execute $\bm a_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)$ and observe the next market return.
    \State Update actual and benchmark accounts; compute $r_t,c_{t+1},\alpha_{t+1}$.
    \State Update required standalone and neutral-replacement accounts on that return.
    \State Store surrogate actions, conditional log probabilities, masses, critic predictions
    \Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent}\hspace{\algorithmicindent}and the reward/cost signals required by $m$.
    \State Reset all accounts only at an episode boundary.
  \EndFor
  \State Bootstrap unfinished episodes and compute GAE with Eq.~\eqref{eq:gae}.
  \State Combine and normalise the estimates in Eq.~\eqref{eq:branch-advantages}.
  \State Update actor and critics over PPO epochs with $\lambda$ fixed.
  \State For RCPO modes, update $\lambda$ once using Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}.
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}

The synthetic generator contains persistent low- and high-volatility regimes, regime-dependent relative winners, mild momentum, and regime-dependent correlations. Assets favoured in calm markets differ from the defensive assets favoured in volatile regimes, forcing the policy to infer the latent state from observations rather than receive a regime label. Training samples uniformly from eight deterministic markets, which reduces direct memorisation of one simulated path. Each 252-step episode represents one trading year and starts from the constrained-neutral allocation.

Every learned policy receives 60,000 updates with 2,048-step rollouts, minibatches of 512, hidden layers of sizes $[192,128]$, discount $0.99$, GAE parameter $0.95$, and proportional transaction cost of one basis point. Gaussian RCPO actors use three PPO epochs per rollout; Gaussian PPO and Dirichlet actors use four. Environment-interaction budgets are equal, but optimisation budgets and learning-rate schedules are not identical across all controls. Return-best checkpoints are selected using mean excess cumulative return on ten fixed validation continuations. Final deterministic evaluation uses the same 20 disjoint future continuations for every policy; no test branch enters model selection. The continuation seeds, anchor state and transaction-cost convention are shared. The comparison concerns the implemented training schemes, including the objective and regularisation differences in Section~\ref{sec:learning}; one policy seed provides no estimate of training-seed uncertainty.

\subsection{Results}

The archived PPO and RCPO configurations show actor-dependent performance. Relative to PPO, Gaussian RCPO records a lower return, from $4.83\%$ to $4.61\%$, and higher MDD, from $17.99\%$ to $19.14\%$; Dirichlet RCPO records a higher return, from $5.58\%$ to $8.06\%$, and lower MDD, from $17.41\%$ to $16.73\%$. The Dirichlet RCPO control wins in 18 of 20 continuations, although only $40\%$ meet its active drawdown-cost target. The soft allocation penalty produces competitive return but no allocation-feasible test branch. Every CAOSD policy records zero allocation violation, consistent with Proposition~1.

Credit-mode rankings depend on the actor. For Gaussian policies, the counterfactual-cost configuration records higher relative wealth, from $4.61\%$ to $4.97\%$, lower mean MDD, from $19.14\%$ to $18.03\%$, and more drawdown-feasible test branches, from $15\%$ to $30\%$. Turnover is also higher: $7.22\%$ rather than $1.91\%$. Counterfactual reward alone is essentially tied with the Gaussian control, and the joint configuration has lower return and feasibility.

For Dirichlet policies, counterfactual reward attains the highest observed mean return ($8.18\%$) and lowest MDD ($16.52\%$) in the comparison. Its paired return difference from the V2.6 control is only $+0.12$ percentage points (approximate $95\%$ paired $t$-interval $[-0.87,+1.10]$), while win rate decreases from $90\%$ to $85\%$ and turnover rises from $14.27\%$ to $24.00\%$. It is therefore not a conclusive return improvement. The corresponding Gaussian MDD difference has an approximate paired interval of $[-1.90,-0.32]$ percentage points, while its turnover increase is $5.31$ percentage points. These intervals describe variation over 20 continuations conditional on one trained seed; they do not quantify training-seed uncertainty. No counterfactual variant uniformly dominates its matched standalone control.

All eight return-best RCPO policies have positive pooled empirical means of $c-\alpha$ on these continuations. Thus none meets the sample analogue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-risk-problem} under deterministic decoding. The feasible-branch rate describes how satisfaction is distributed across test paths; it does not replace this pooled residual statistic or estimate the constraint value of the stochastic training policy.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The distinction between isolated branch performance and contribution to the combined allocation is substantive even before comparing learning curves. Equation~\eqref{eq:coupling-identity} shows why a branch can affect mass outside its own term. Equation~\eqref{eq:history-identity} shows why a pathwise difference can persist after current allocations coincide. These relations establish what the credit signal measures. Whether that signal improves learning remains an empirical question.

The archived comparison suggests that the answer can depend on the actor distribution and the performance criterion. Counterfactual reward has the highest observed Dirichlet mean return, but its paired interval includes zero and turnover increases. The joint reward--cost variant offers no consistent advantage. Weighting, entropy and critic-information differences prevent attributing these patterns to the difference signal alone. In particular, matching an entropy coefficient does not match exploration when its branch weights and coordinate distributions differ.

The risk results require a separate interpretation. Allocation feasibility follows from the action construction, while all eight return-best RCPO policies miss the pooled residual-cost target under deterministic evaluation. No V3.2 return-best policy exceeds a $45\%$ test feasible-branch rate. A separate checkpoint rule that prioritises validation feasibility before return also reaches at most $45\%$ test feasibility; for counterfactual-cost Dirichlet, test relative wealth falls to $0.92\%$. Selection can change the operating point without supplying an out-of-sample risk guarantee. Current drawdown cost and maximum drawdown also measure different aspects of a trajectory: similar maximum losses can coexist with different durations and depths of threshold exceedance.

Three evidence gaps limit the empirical claim. First, the archived maximum-drawdown summaries must be regenerated with initial wealth included. Second, credit modes need comparison under matched branch surrogates, mass weighting, entropy conventions, optimisation budgets and a documented critic-information protocol, followed by independent training seeds. Third, the interpretation suggested by the analysis needs direct diagnostics: remapped mass changes, immediate versus inherited-history contrasts, replacement sensitivity and training overhead. These are requirements for testing the credit mechanism, rather than results inferred from the current return rankings.

The general formulation does not by itself establish transfer across allocation domains. Exogenous market returns make the replacement accounting available here; action-dependent external dynamics need a suitable transition model. Historical walk-forward evaluation and richer execution accounting are also needed before drawing conclusions about financial use. The present results depend on one trained seed, a deliberately learnable synthetic generator, fixed validation paths and deterministic decoding. Repeated validation selection can add selection bias. Appendix~\ref{app:evidence} records the outstanding validation tasks without treating them as completed experiments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies branch credit in sequential constrained allocation with adjustment costs and path-dependent risk. An established CAOSD map enforces the simplex and two group limits, while an RCPO-inspired multiplier adapts the pressure on average path cost. Within this architecture, feasible neutral replacements define shadow trajectories whose differences can be used for branch learning.

The analysis distinguishes redistribution caused by the allocation map from consequences inherited through account history. It also identifies why retaining downstream autoregressive samples does not generally yield a gradient-preserving baseline. These distinctions make the implemented credit signals and their limitations explicit.

The portfolio experiments provide conditional evidence about complete training schemes. Counterfactual-reward Dirichlet has the highest observed mean relative wealth without a conclusive improvement over its control, and the pooled path-cost target remains unmet. Controlled ablations, repeated training and corrected drawdown summaries are needed to determine whether the credit construction yields a reproducible learning advantage.

\section{Feasibility of the Two-Group CAOSD Map}
\label{app:feasibility}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility}]
For completeness, we verify the stated feasibility property of the CAOSD construction \citep{winkel2023}. Since $\ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1]$,
$0\leq z_1\leq\min\{\ell_1,\ell_2\}$ and $z_2=\ell_1-z_1\geq0$.
A non-empty second branch gives $q_2\in[0,1]$; an empty branch gives $q_2=0$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  0\leq z_3
     \leq\ell_2-z_1
     \leq1-\ell_1,\qquad
  z_4=1-\ell_1-z_3\geq0.
  \label{eq:proof-mass-bound}
\end{equation}
The upper bound uses
$\ell_2-z_1=\min\{\ell_2,1-\ell_1\}$.
The coefficients sum to one by definition.

It remains to check that an empty branch cannot remove positive mass from the allocation. Non-emptiness of the feasible set excludes $V_k=\varnothing$ whenever $\ell_k>0$. Thus $K_2=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_1=z_1=z_2=0$, while $K_3=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_2=z_1=z_3=0$. If $K_1=\varnothing$, the groups are disjoint; feasibility then requires $\ell_1+\ell_2\leq1$, giving $z_1=0$. Since $K_4=\mathcal E$ is non-empty, every positive coefficient multiplies a non-negative unit-mass branch. Branches with zero coefficients contribute nothing, regardless of whether their supports are empty. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf T(\bm u)\geq\bm0,\qquad
  \mathbf1^\top\mathsf T(\bm u)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i=1.
  \label{eq:proof-simplex}
\end{equation}
The prescribed supports further imply
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in V_1}a_j\geq z_1+z_2=\ell_1,\qquad
  \sum_{j\in V_2}a_j\geq z_1+z_2q_2+z_3\geq\ell_2.
  \label{eq:proof-exposure}
\end{equation}
All omitted contributions to either group are non-negative. This proves simplex membership and both exposure bounds, including overlapping groups, disjoint groups and zero-mass branches.\end{proof}

In floating-point arithmetic, the implementation clips negligible negative round-off and normalises the final allocation. Numerical feasibility diagnostics are interpreted relative to their tolerance.

\section{Portfolio Parameters and Numerical Conventions}
\label{app:instance}

\subsection{Instance parameters}

With the simulator's zero-based entity identifiers, the experimental groups and minimum exposures are
\begin{equation}
  V_1=\{1,4,6\},\quad \ell_1=0.50;\qquad
  V_2=\{2,4,7\},\quad \ell_2=0.40.
  \label{eq:portfolio-groups}
\end{equation}
These identifiers describe the instance without fixing $N$ in the general formulation. Since $\ell_1+\ell_2<1$, the first coefficient is identically zero and branch~1 receives no policy update. Branches~2--4 remain trainable; the overlap still influences $q_2$ and $z_3$. Agent and benchmark start at the CAOSD image of uniform branch weights. The benchmark retains this target and incurs neither an initial target-change fee nor subsequent target-change fees under the simulator's accounting convention.

The parameters in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth}--\eqref{eq:portfolio-alpha} and \eqref{eq:lambda} are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    \kappa=10^{-4},\qquad b_{\min}=0.05,\qquad
    \beta=0.90,\qquad s_C=0.10,\qquad \nu=0.05,\\
    \varepsilon_R=10^{-6},\qquad \varepsilon_W=10^{-12},\qquad
    \eta_\uparrow=0.00075,\qquad\eta_\downarrow=0.01,\qquad\lambda_0=0.
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:instance-parameters}
\end{equation}
The benchmark adapts the drawdown threshold and hence the cost allowance. The multiplier responds once per rollout to the realised mean residual; neither mechanism projects the portfolio onto a path-risk-feasible set. Current drawdown determines the training cost, while maximum drawdown is retained as a diagnostic and evaluation statistic.

\subsection{Accounting and numerical safeguards}

For two probability vectors,
$T_t\leq\lVert\bm w_t\rVert_1+\lVert\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1=2$.
Moreover, the portfolio's gross return is a convex combination of entity gross returns. Hence
\begin{equation}
  g_{t+1}\geq
     \min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})-2\kappa.
  \label{eq:positive-growth-bound}
\end{equation}
The condition $\min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})>2\kappa$ is sufficient for positive net growth. It is not implied by simplex membership, and the Gaussian return model does not guarantee it. Numerical floors keep individual updates defined, but do not model liquidation, default or an economically self-financing recovery. If either floor is active, reward need not equal the log wealth increment. In exact arithmetic, positive floored growth and $W_0=W_0^B=1$ keep both running peaks at least one and drawdown denominators non-zero; this does not rule out floating-point underflow over arbitrary horizons.

Exact holdings-level self-financing would require measuring trades from return-drifted pre-trade holdings and accounting for fee payment when determining post-trade wealth and weights \citep{jiang2017}. Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth} instead deducts a proportional target-change proxy. Full-vector turnover includes reserve transfers, but slippage, tiered fees and market impact are absent. A constant-target benchmark is also not charged for rebalancing caused by holding drift. The empirical results must be read under these accounting assumptions.

For numerical PPO evaluation, joint and conditional log-probability differences are clipped to $[-20,20]$ before exponentiation. Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-ratios} gives the unmodified ratios, recovered when this safeguard is inactive. For any combined advantage vector $A$ over a rollout, normalisation is
$\widetilde A=(A-\operatorname{mean}(A))/(\operatorname{sd}(A)+10^{-8})$,
where $\operatorname{sd}$ is the population standard deviation. Branches are normalised separately after combining reward and cost. Actual, benchmark and auxiliary path memories persist across rollout boundaries and reset at episode boundaries.

\subsection{Policy independence of the benchmark allowance}

Let $\mathbb P_x$ denote the law of the exogenous market trajectory. Market sampling, horizon $H$, benchmark target and initial benchmark state are fixed independently of $\theta$. Its allowance is therefore a functional $\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})$ of that trajectory. For every admissible allocation policy,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]
      &=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_x}\!\left[
        \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})\right],\\
    \nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]&=\bm0.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:allowance-independence}
\end{equation}
The expectation is constant in $\theta$, so no interchange of differentiation and integration is needed. Fixed discount weights give the same policy-independence property. This explains why the global critic can omit the allowance from its policy-dependent cost target. It does not remove the actor--dual discount mismatch or make the branch objectives exact Lagrangian gradients.

\section{A Retained Downstream Sample Need Not Be a Valid Baseline}
\label{app:baseline}

A scalar example isolates the dependence introduced by autoregressive sampling. It is an analytical illustration, not an additional portfolio experiment. Let a first variable $U_1$ and a conditional second variable $U_2$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  U_1\sim\mathcal N(\theta,1),\qquad
  U_2\mid U_1\sim\mathcal N(U_1,1),\qquad
  F(U_1,U_2)=U_1+U_2.
  \label{eq:baseline-example}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, $\theta$ is a scalar policy parameter, and $F$ is the outcome. The conditional kernel of $U_2$ has no explicit dependence on $\theta$ when $U_1$ is held fixed.

The expected outcome is $2\theta$, so its derivative is $2$. Replacing $U_1$ by zero while retaining realised $U_2$ gives the baseline $F(0,U_2)=U_2$ and difference $U_1$. The first-variable log-density derivative is $U_1-\theta$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E[(U_1-\theta)F(U_1,U_2)]=2,\qquad
  \mathbb E[(U_1-\theta)\{F(U_1,U_2)-F(0,U_2)\}]=1.
  \label{eq:baseline-counterexample}
\end{equation}
The baseline term has non-zero score expectation because $U_2$ depends on $U_1$. A fixed downstream sample therefore cannot generally be treated as an action-independent baseline for an earlier autoregressive decision. This example does not imply that difference credit cannot be useful; it shows why its learning effect needs evidence rather than an automatic unbiasedness claim.

\section{Outstanding Empirical Validation}
\label{app:evidence}

The following checks are not completed by the archived comparison:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Re-evaluate the stored checkpoints, or recompute from complete wealth series, with initial wealth included in maximum drawdown. Verify checkpoint identities, fees, benchmark and residual-cost summaries together.
  \item Compare shared, standalone and counterfactual credit under a common conditional-branch surrogate and matched optimisation budgets. Separate credit changes from mass weighting, entropy weighting and critic information.
  \item Repeat training with independent seeds and report uncertainty over trained policies as well as shared test continuations. Keep checkpoint selection on validation data.
  \item Test the explanations in Section~\ref{sec:credit-analysis} through overlap and remapping diagnostics, replacement sensitivity, and current-action versus inherited-history contrasts.
  \item Measure runtime, memory and critic overhead. Assess robustness on historical walk-forward data and, for empirical cross-domain claims, an additional allocation task.
\end{enumerate}
These tasks define the evidence needed to assess reproducible benefit and wider applicability. No improvement or risk-feasibility result is assumed for them.
