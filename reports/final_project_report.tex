\author{\name Housen Zhu\thanks{OpenAI Codex assisted with manuscript revision, mathematical exposition, notation checks and LaTeX preparation.}\\
\addr Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering\\
\addr University of Toronto}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A feasible resource allocation can be assembled from several policy branches, but the combined outcome gives each branch the same feedback. We study branch credit when the allocation map couples branch decisions and costs depend on earlier allocations. Our architecture uses the two-group Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition map, Proximal Policy Optimization and an adaptive multiplier inspired by Reward Constrained Policy Optimization. For counterfactual credit, we replace one branch with a neutral choice, recompute the feasible allocation and compare the two actions from the same realised history. We show how the replacement redistributes resources and why the retained downstream samples can prevent the reference from being an unbiased policy-gradient baseline. In a synthetic portfolio experiment, we compare credit modes across three training seeds and 100 shared held-out continuations. The cost-only counterfactual variant has the highest mean relative wealth, but its paired gain over standalone is $0.19$ percentage points with a 95\% interval of $[-0.37,0.89]$. Its mean path-cost gap increases. All simplex-mapped policies satisfy the allocation limits, and all learned policies exceed the mean path-cost budget under return-based checkpoint selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Contributions}
\label{sec:introduction}

Sequential allocation assigns a limited resource among competing entities while accounting for consequences that unfold over time. A computing system may trade immediate throughput against migration costs; an investor may trade expected growth against transaction costs and drawdown. In both cases, an allocation must be admissible when executed, while its value depends on the sequence of allocations and outcomes. Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a way to learn these intertemporal trade-offs.

Admissibility and trajectory performance impose different requirements. Non-negativity, resource conservation and group limits restrict the current action. A cumulative risk limit restricts the outcomes of a policy over time. Enforcing one does not enforce the other: a portfolio can respect every exposure limit and still experience an unacceptable drawdown. Conversely, an acceptable average cost does not by itself exclude an inadmissible allocation. This distinction motivates a feasible action construction coupled to adaptive learning of path costs.

A further question arises when the construction decomposes an allocation into several learned branches: \emph{which branch should receive credit for the realised outcome?} A branch evaluated in isolation need not have the same value inside the combined allocation. Its mass may be small, its choice may change the masses assigned to other branches, and the final adjustment cost depends on the combined action. With path-dependent costs, two otherwise identical allocations can also produce different losses because their preceding allocations and wealth histories differ. Credit therefore depends on both the allocation map and the trajectory state.

This paper studies that problem within a concrete, implemented architecture. Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition (CAOSD) supplies feasible actions under two possibly overlapping group limits \citep{winkel2023}. An autoregressive policy selects the branch variables, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) updates their distributions \citep{schulman2017}. A multiplier inspired by Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) adjusts the penalty on realised path cost \citep{tessler2019}. Counterfactual (CF) credit compares the executed allocation with a feasible allocation obtained by replacing one branch with a fixed neutral choice. Both allocations are evaluated on the same transition from the actual history.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}

Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) express reward maximisation subject to expected cost limits \citep{kushwaha2025}. Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) develops policy updates using bounds on reward and cost changes \citep{achiam2017}; RCPO uses a learned penalty to guide policy improvement \citep{tessler2019}. These approaches address expected outcomes. Action-constrained RL instead restricts each action to an admissible set \citep{lin2021}. For allocations, CAOSD constructs feasible actions through coupled simplex-valued branches, while Polytope Action Space Policy Optimization (PASPO) addresses more general linear constraints \citep{winkel2023,winkel2024paspo}. We use the two-group CAOSD map and retain that restriction throughout.

Credit assignment is a separate line of work. Counterfactual multi-agent policy gradients use an action-marginalised baseline to assess individual decisions \citep{foerster2018}. Difference rewards compare outcomes after replacing one component and can exploit a known reward model \citep{castellini2025}. In our setting the components are conditional branches of one policy, and a replacement can change the mixing weights of branches whose choices are held fixed. Both features matter when interpreting the resulting difference.

Portfolio allocation makes these dependencies explicit through transaction costs and wealth-based risk \citep{jiang2017,gollart2025}. More broadly, combining a known structural model with sequential learning can reduce the decisions left to the policy, as illustrated in resource-control applications such as \citet{ding2021}. Here the structural model establishes allocation feasibility; it does not solve the trajectory optimisation problem.

\subsection{Scope and contributions}

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item It specifies a constrained allocation problem that separates hard action support from expected path cost, including the allocation and risk memory required by the environment and the information actually supplied to the policy.
  \item It defines one-step neutral-replacement credit for dependent allocation branches, derives the induced redistribution of allocation mass, and identifies the limitation of using retained downstream choices as a baseline.
  \item It evaluates shared, standalone and one-step counterfactual credit under a matched training protocol in a portfolio instance, using shared held-out paths and repeated training seeds.
\end{enumerate}
CAOSD, PPO, RCPO and difference-based credit retain their original attribution. The research focus is their interaction in a stateful allocation problem. The model is stated for general allocation, and the matched empirical comparison is specific to the portfolio environment.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

Sequential allocation concerns a resource that must be divided among several entities as conditions change. The decision is not simply which allocation performs best at the present moment. Moving away from an earlier allocation can incur a cost, and the outcomes accumulated so far can change how the next decision should be assessed. A policy must therefore account for both the allocation it chooses now and the history from which that choice is made. We distinguish requirements that every allocation must satisfy from limits on costs accumulated over a trajectory.

The portfolio environment gives these ideas a concrete meaning. Resource shares become investment weights, changing those weights incurs a transaction-cost proxy, and the resulting wealth history determines drawdown. Market outcomes are external to the investor, whereas transaction costs and risk depend on the investor's decisions. We first specify the general decision process and its constrained objective, then give the accounting rules needed to evaluate alternative allocations on a realised transition.

\subsection{Sequential Allocation and Information Structure}

Consider a divisible resource distributed among $N$ entities over $H$ decision periods. The entity set is $\mathcal E=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$, and decisions are indexed by $t=0,\ldots,H-1$. An action $\bm w_t$ specifies the resource share assigned to each entity. At the start of a period, the policy observes the information available at that time and selects an allocation. The environment then produces an outcome, after which the reward, costs and internal state are updated. Decisions cannot use outcomes that have not yet occurred.

We describe the complete environment by a finite-horizon constrained Markov decision process (CMDP),
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,\mathcal R,\gamma,\mathcal C).
  \label{eq:allocation-mdp}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal S$ is the state space and $\mathcal A$ is the allocation space. The transition kernel $P$ specifies the distribution of the next state conditional on the current state and action. The reward function $\mathcal R$ assigns a scalar reward to that transition, and $\gamma\in(0,1]$ determines the weight placed on future rewards, i.e., discount factor. The collection $\mathcal C$ contains $Q$ transition-cost functions, denoted by $C_q$ for $q=1,\ldots,Q$. The horizon $H$ and initial-state distribution $\mu_0$ complete the specification. Reward and cost returns are assumed integrable under the policies considered.

Reallocation costs and path-dependent risk require information beyond current external conditions. The same target allocation can incur different reallocation costs depending on the previous allocation, even when external conditions are unchanged. The state must therefore retain the previous allocation to determine the cost of reallocation. Similarly, risk measures defined over a trajectory require sufficient statistics of past outcomes. Together, these variables provide the memory needed to calculate rewards and costs as new outcomes are realised.

We therefore write the complete state as
\begin{equation}
  s_t=(x_t,\bm w_{t-1},\zeta_t,t).
  \label{eq:augmented-state}
\end{equation}
The component $x_t$ contains the external variables and history sufficient to describe their evolution. The previous target is $\bm w_{t-1}$, while $\zeta_t$ collects the remaining internal statistics needed to update outcomes and path risk. Time is included because the remaining decision horizon can affect the value of an action. We assume this complete state is Markov: once $s_t$ and $\bm w_t$ are given, earlier history provides no further information about the next-state distribution,
\begin{equation}
  s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_t,\bm w_t).
  \label{eq:allocation-transition}
\end{equation}
Write $h_t=(\bm w_{t-1},\zeta_t)$ for the agent's internal memory within $s_t$. The portfolio instance below specifies the account statistics it contains. When training uses a standalone branch reward, the simulator also stores the previous sampled sub-allocations for computing branch-level adjustment costs (Section~\ref{sec:feasible-rcpo}). This extra memory is used to construct training targets; the observation describes the executed allocation and its outcomes.

The complete state used to define the environment need not be fully visible to the policy. Let $\mathsf O$ be the observation map, so that the policy receives
\begin{equation}
  o_t=\mathsf O(s_t).
  \label{eq:allocation-observation}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal F_t$ denote all information revealed before the decision, including observations, earlier actions and realised rewards and costs. A general non-anticipative policy $\pi_t(\cdot\mid\mathcal F_t)$ may use this available history, but cannot use future outcomes. In our implementation, the feedforward actor selects actions from the current observation \(o_t\), which combines a finite window of market returns with allocation and risk statistics. These statistics retain the information needed to calculate reallocation costs and update path risk. They do not reveal the latent market regime, so the actor operates under partial observation.

\subsection{Feasible Actions and Constrained Objective}

The first requirement is resource conservation. Each action must allocate the whole resource without assigning a negative share:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A=\AllocSimplex
    :=\left\{\bm w\in\R^N:
       w_j\geq0\ \text{for all }j\in\mathcal E,\
       \mathbf1^\top\bm w=1\right\}.
  \label{eq:allocation-simplex}
\end{equation}
The superscript $N$ counts allocation coordinates, and $\mathbf1$ is the vector of ones. Additional requirements can reserve a minimum share for a group of entities. For  prescribed groups $V_1,\ldots, V_K\subseteq\mathcal E$ and minimum shares $\ell_1,\ldots, \ell_K\in[0,1]$, the hard-feasible set is
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{F}_k: \sum_{j\in V_k}w_j\geq\ell_k
\end{align}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\mathrm h}
    =\left\{\bm w\in\AllocSimplex:
       \sum_{j\in V_k}w_j\geq\ell_k,\quad k=1,\ldots, K\right\}.
  \label{eq:hard-action-set}
\end{equation}
The groups may overlap, in which case a share assigned to their intersection contributes towards both requirements. We assume the limits are mutually feasible. A maximum share $u$ for one group can also be expressed as a minimum share $1-u$ for its complement, because all shares sum to one. The implemented construction handles two group inequalities of this form.

These requirements apply when an action is executed. Define $\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ as the class of non-anticipative policies whose action distributions satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t(\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}\mid\mathcal F_t)=1
  \quad\text{almost surely for every }t=0,\ldots,H-1.
  \label{eq:hard-feasibility}
\end{equation}
This condition makes the distinction between feasible sampling and penalised learning precise. Softmax and Dirichlet constructions can produce simplex-valued samples, but they do not by themselves enforce the additional group limits. A finite violation penalty changes the desirability of an action without restricting its support. Exactly zero expected non-negative violation would imply zero violation almost surely; a small estimated penalty during learning does not establish that condition. We therefore impose allocation feasibility structurally and use the cumulative-cost objective for consequences that depend on a trajectory.

Let $r_t=\mathcal R(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})$ denote the realised transition reward. For a policy $\pi$, expected discounted reward is
\begin{equation}
  J_R(\pi)
    =\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^t r_t\right].
  \label{eq:reward-return}
\end{equation}
The expectation includes initial-state, transition and policy randomness. For each cost function, define the expected episode-average cost as
\begin{equation}
  J_{C_q}(\pi)
    =\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}C_q(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})\right].
  \label{eq:cost-return}
\end{equation}
Averaging gives every period the same weight in the cost requirement; the reward objective separately uses $\gamma$ to discount future utility. Given a fixed limit $d_q$ for each of the $Q$ cost functions, the allocation problem is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}}\quad &J_R(\pi)\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
      &J_{C_q}(\pi)\leq d_q.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hybrid-problem}
\end{equation}

The two parts of this problem constrain different objects. Membership in $\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ makes every selected allocation admissible. The cost inequalities limit the policy's expected cumulative consequences, allowing costs to vary across periods and trajectories. This constraint limits the expected average cost over the horizon. Individual periods and realised trajectories may still exceed the corresponding cost target. The following portfolio instance specifies how a history-dependent financial risk can enter this formulation as a transition cost.

\subsection{Portfolio Environment with Drawdown Costs}

In the portfolio instance, the resource is investment capital. The action $\bm w_t$ specifies target portfolio weights, where $w_{t,j}$ is the target fraction of capital allocated to asset $j$. Coordinate $0$ represents cash, which earns zero return in the model. The investor chooses $\bm w_t$ at time $t$, before observing the returns over the following period. The vector of simple asset returns is
\begin{equation}
  \bm R_{t+1}
    =\bigl(0,R_{t+1,1},\ldots,R_{t+1,N-1}\bigr)^\top
    \in\R^N.
  \label{eq:asset-return-vector}
\end{equation}
For a non-cash asset with price $p_{t,j}>0$ at time $t$, its simple return is the fractional price change,
\begin{equation}
  R_{t+1,j}
    =\frac{p_{t+1,j}-p_{t,j}}{p_{t,j}},
  \qquad j=1,\ldots,N-1.
  \label{eq:asset-simple-return}
\end{equation}
The cash entry is zero. The asset-return vector $\bm R_{t+1}$ is distinct from the scalar reward $r_t$ and reward function $\mathcal R$. These expressions specify the financial meaning of the return vector; the synthetic environment generates returns directly rather than computing them from observed prices. Market returns are assumed to be unaffected by the investor's allocation.

Changing the target incurs a fee proportional to turnover. The simulator measures that turnover by the full-vector change from the preceding target,
\begin{equation}
  T_t=\lVert\bm w_t-\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1.
  \label{eq:portfolio-turnover}
\end{equation}
The norm sums absolute weight changes, including transfers to and from the cash entry. With fee coefficient $\kappa\geq0$, the resulting net growth factor is
\begin{equation}
  g_{t+1}=1+\bm w_t^\top\bm R_{t+1}-\kappa T_t.
  \label{eq:portfolio-net-growth}
\end{equation}
Thus the same market return can produce different net outcomes when reached through different reallocations. Starting from normalised wealth $W_0=1$, the account evolves according to
\begin{equation}
  W_{t+1}=W_t\max\{\varepsilon_W,g_{t+1}\}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-wealth}
\end{equation}
The transition reward is the portfolio’s one-period net log return, with a positive floor applied to the growth factor for numerical stability,
\begin{equation}
  r_t=\mathcal R(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})
     =\log\!\bigl(\max\{\varepsilon_R,g_{t+1}\}\bigr).
  \label{eq:portfolio-reward}
\end{equation}
The positive constants $\varepsilon_W$ and $\varepsilon_R$ are numerical floors. When both are inactive, the reward equals the change in log wealth.

Transaction costs are calculated from changes in target weights; changes in actual portfolio weights caused by market returns are not included in this calculation. The simulator omits slippage, tiered fees and market impact, and does not charge the constant-target benchmark for rebalancing caused by holding drift. Appendix~\ref{app:instance} gives the numerical constants, initial allocations and a sufficient condition for positive net growth. These conventions determine the financial interpretation of both rewards and risk.

The risk cost asks how far wealth has fallen below a previously attained level. The running wealth peak is
\begin{equation}
  M_t=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}W_v.
  \label{eq:portfolio-peak}
\end{equation}
Here $v$ indexes elapsed periods, including the initial account value. Current drawdown is the fractional shortfall from that peak,
\begin{equation}
  D_t=1-\frac{W_t}{M_t}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-drawdown}
\end{equation}
Maximum drawdown records the largest such shortfall observed so far,
\begin{equation}
  D_t^{\max}=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}D_v.
  \label{eq:portfolio-max-drawdown}
\end{equation}
A positive return can reduce current drawdown without eliminating it, because recovering part of a previous loss need not restore the earlier wealth peak. This is why the next return alone cannot determine the risk cost. The account's wealth and peak must also be retained. Current drawdown determines the training cost below; maximum drawdown remains an observation and evaluation statistic.

A constrained-neutral benchmark follows a fixed feasible target on the same market returns. Superscript $B$ denotes its wealth, peak and drawdown statistics, and its initial wealth and peak are both one. The benchmark provides a reference for how severe losses are on the shared market path. The investor's drawdown is still measured relative to its own peak, but the threshold against which it is assessed adapts to the benchmark:
\begin{equation}
  b_{t+1}=\max\{b_{\min},\beta D_{t+1}^B\}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-risk-threshold}
\end{equation}
The parameter $b_{\min}>0$ supplies a positive threshold floor, while $\beta>0$ controls the response to benchmark drawdown. The cost charges only the amount by which current drawdown exceeds that threshold,
\begin{equation}
  c_{t+1}=\frac{[D_{t+1}-b_{t+1}]_+^2}{s_C}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-cost}
\end{equation}
The notation $[x]_+$ means $\max(0,x)$, and $s_C>0$ scales the squared excess. Squaring makes deeper exceedances more costly, while repeated exceedances accumulate through the episode-average objective. A cost allowance on the same scale is
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{t+1}=\frac{(\nu b_{t+1})^2}{s_C}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-alpha}
\end{equation}
The non-negative parameter $\nu$ sets this allowance relative to the moving threshold. The two quantities have separate roles: $b_{t+1}$ is a drawdown level, whereas $\alpha_{t+1}$ specifies the cost level used in the average constraint.

All of these updates follow the decision timing introduced above. At time \(t\), the policy chooses \(\boldsymbol w_t\) using the current observation, which includes the threshold \(b_t\). After the return \(\boldsymbol R_{t+1}\) is observed, the portfolio and benchmark wealth and drawdown are updated. The new threshold \(b_{t+1}\), cost \(c_{t+1}\) and allowance \(\alpha_{t+1}\) are then calculated from these updated quantities.

The internal memory needed by this account model and its diagnostics is
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_t=(W_t,M_t,D_t^{\max},
           W_t^B,M_t^B,D_t^{B,\max},T_{t-1}).
  \label{eq:portfolio-path-state}
\end{equation}
Wealth and peaks recover current drawdowns, while the other entries retain maximum drawdowns and previous turnover for the observation. Together with the previous allocation and external state, these quantities support recursive updates without replaying the complete trading history. Since the next market return and benchmark threshold are shared across allocations, an alternative action can be evaluated for one period from the same pre-decision memory $h_t$. Its temporary wealth and peak follow Eqs.~\eqref{eq:portfolio-wealth} and \eqref{eq:portfolio-peak}, starting at $W_t$ and $M_t$.

The actual observation provides market information through trailing returns and their rolling means and volatility. It describes the investor's position through previous weights, turnover and group-exposure diagnostics, and reports the current risk quantities, threshold gap and elapsed fraction of the horizon. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} details these components and their sources.

Finally, the portfolio risk requirement is obtained from the general constrained objective by using one centred transition cost,
\begin{equation}
  C_1(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})=c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1}.
  \label{eq:portfolio-centred-cost}
\end{equation}
Its fixed limit is $d_1=0$, giving
\begin{equation}
  J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi)
    :=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
       \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}
       (c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1})\right]\leq0.
  \label{eq:adaptive-risk-problem}
\end{equation}
Keeping the random allowance inside the expectation makes clear that the requirement concerns average residual cost under a benchmark-dependent tolerance. The hard allocation limits remain unchanged throughout the trajectory. Maximum drawdown and terminal relative wealth, measured by $W_H/W_H^B-1$, provide additional portfolio evaluation statistics. Section~\ref{sec:learning} describes how the learner approximates this constrained objective using branch advantages and an adaptive multiplier.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\label{sec:reformulation}

RCPO adjusts the pressure on expected cost, and simplex decomposition enforces feasibility when an allocation is sampled. Together they give a feasible policy with an adaptive cost penalty. The remaining question is how to assign feedback to the branches that jointly produce an allocation.

\subsection{Allocation with RCPO Alone}

One way to approach Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid-problem} is to sample directly from the simplex $\mathcal A$ and handle the remaining constraints through costs. The functions $C_q$ already measure the costs incurred along a trajectory. The group limits can be represented by an additional non-negative cost,
\begin{equation}
  v(\bm w)=\sum_{k=1}^K
      \left[\ell_k-\sum_{j\in V_k}w_j\right]_+,
  \label{eq:group-shortfall}
\end{equation}
where $[x]_+=\max(0,x)$. This cost sums the shortfalls across groups. A zero budget for its expected episode average is equivalent to satisfying every group limit almost surely, since all shortfalls are non-negative and the horizon is finite.

RCPO adapts the strength of a cost penalty during learning \citep{tessler2019}. For an expected cost $J_C(\pi)$ with limit $d_C$, the reward-maximisation Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)
    =J_R(\pi)-\lambda\bigl(J_C(\pi)-d_C\bigr),
  \qquad \lambda\geq0.
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
In RCPO, a critic estimates the discounted return of $r_t-\lambda c_t^{\mathrm{guide}}$, where $c_t^{\mathrm{guide}}$ is a transition-level penalty chosen to reflect the constraint. The actor uses that estimate to improve its choices. A slower update adjusts the multiplier $\lambda$ using the original constraint criterion. In the finite-horizon notation of Section~\ref{sec:model}, a representative update is
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\leftarrow
    [\lambda+\eta_\lambda(\widehat J_C-d_C)]_+,
  \label{eq:rcpo-background-update}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat J_C$ is an estimate of $J_C(\pi)$ from sampled trajectories and $\eta_\lambda>0$ is the multiplier step size. Estimated costs above the limit raise the penalty, while estimates below the limit allow it to decrease. The same design can assign a separate multiplier to each budget.

This design lets the actor learn from transition feedback while the penalty adapts to the episode cost budget. The update acts on expected costs and leaves the policy free to sample from $\mathcal A$. Even a small estimated group shortfall can therefore coexist with violating actions during training. The convergence result for RCPO concerns asymptotic satisfaction under assumptions about the guiding penalty and the updates. To enforce the group limits from the first decision, we can instead construct a policy whose samples already lie in $\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}$.

\subsection{Allocation with Simplex Decomposition Alone}

A second approach builds the group limits into the action representation and trains the policy to maximise $J_R$ over the resulting allocations. For the case $K=2$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hard-action-set}, CAOSD handles two possibly overlapping groups \citep{winkel2023}. The learner samples a surrogate action $\bm u_t$, and the environment executes the allocation given by the deterministic map
\begin{equation}
  \bm w_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}.
  \label{eq:surrogate-map}
\end{equation}
The four branch supports specify which entities each branch can allocate to:
\begin{equation}
  K_1=V_1\cap V_2,\qquad K_2=V_1,\qquad
  K_3=V_2,\qquad K_4=\mathcal E.
  \label{eq:caosd-branches}
\end{equation}
Branch indices are $i=1,\ldots,4$, while $j$ indexes entities. Each surrogate component $\bm u_i$ determines a non-negative vector $\bm y_i\in\R^N$. Its entries sum to one on a non-empty $K_i$ and are zero outside $K_i$. An empty support is represented by $\bm y_i=\bm0$. Define branch~2's overlap share as
\begin{equation}
  q_2=\sum_{j\in V_1\cap V_2}y_{2,j}.
  \label{eq:q2}
\end{equation}
The mixing masses and resulting allocation are
\begin{align}
  z_1&=[\ell_1+\ell_2-1]_+,&
  z_2&=[\ell_1-z_1]_+,\nonumber\\
  z_3&=[\ell_2-z_1-z_2q_2]_+,&
  z_4&=1-z_1-z_2-z_3,
  \label{eq:caosd-masses}\\
  \mathsf T(\bm u)
    &=\CAOSD(\bm y_1,\ldots,\bm y_4)
      =\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i\bm y_i.
  \label{eq:caosd}
\end{align}
Branch~1 supplies mandatory mass to the intersection. Branch~2 completes the first-group requirement, branch~3 covers the remaining second-group requirement, and branch~4 distributes the residual. A time subscript denotes the masses induced by the branches sampled at decision $t$.

\begin{proposition}[Two-group CAOSD feasibility; \citealp{winkel2023}]
\label{prop:feasibility}
For $K=2$, suppose $\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}$ is non-empty and every branch with non-empty support is a probability vector on that support. Then $z_i\geq0$, $\sum_i z_i=1$, and every branch with empty support has zero coefficient. The allocation $\mathsf T(\bm u)$ satisfies the simplex and both group limits in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:allocation-simplex} and \eqref{eq:hard-action-set}.
\end{proposition}
Appendix~\ref{app:feasibility} gives the proof, including disjoint groups and zero-mass branches. The guarantee is algebraic and therefore holds independently of policy convergence or critic accuracy.

For two group limits, the map guarantees the action requirement in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hard-feasibility} from the first decision. A policy trained on reward alone can still exceed the trajectory budgets in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid-problem}. The decomposition also couples the branches. A change in branch~2 can alter $q_2$ and redistribute mass between branches~3 and~4. The combined reward and cost give no reference for assessing how that branch changed the outcome. The next subsection uses branch replacements to define such a reference. Section~\ref{sec:learning} brings this credit together with feasible sampling and an adaptive penalty on trajectory cost.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Counterfactual Credit for Allocation Branches}

The branches can be viewed as virtual agents whose choices contribute to a single action. A shared learning signal scores the combined allocation. Standalone credit scores each sub-allocation in isolation, leaving out interactions introduced by $\mathsf T$. Counterfactual credit asks how the combined outcome changes when the choice of one branch is replaced.

This comparison draws on multi-agent credit assignment. Counterfactual multi-agent policy gradients use a critic baseline that marginalises one agent's action while holding the others fixed \citep{foerster2018}. Difference rewards can evaluate a reference action through a known reward model \citep{castellini2025}. Here the branches belong to one autoregressive policy, so a later branch's sampled choice can depend on an earlier choice. That dependence matters when a sample is held fixed during replacement.

Suppose the task allows the reward or cost of an alternative action to be evaluated from the real pre-decision state under the same external outcome. This is possible in an exogenous environment with known internal update rules. Replace branch $i$ with a reference on its support, retain the other sampled branches, and recompute the entire feasible map. Recomputing allows the mixing masses to respond to the replacement, and Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility} applies to both actions. For a transition signal $f$, let $f_t^{\mathrm{act}}$ be the realised value and $f_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}}$ its one-step value under the replacement. The branch signal is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta f_{i,t}=f_t^{\mathrm{act}}-f_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}}.
  \label{eq:general-counterfactual-credit}
\end{equation}
A positive reward difference favours the executed choice in this comparison, while a positive cost difference records an increase in cost. Collecting these one-step signals along the actual rollout lets the learner use generalised advantage estimation (GAE) \citep{schulman2015gae}. The resulting advantages are used with the PPO clipping function \citep{schulman2017},
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_\epsilon(\rho,A)
    =\min\{\rho A,\operatorname{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A\},
  \label{eq:ppo-surrogate}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the input advantage, $\rho$ is the updated-to-rollout likelihood ratio for the sampled branch choice, and $\epsilon>0$ sets the clipping width. Section~\ref{sec:learning} specifies the standalone and counterfactual signals and how each enters the branch update.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:learning}

Simplex decomposition enforces the group limits at each decision, while an adaptive penalty responds to the trajectory cost budget. We first describe how the actor samples feasible allocations and how the penalty is updated. We then compare shared, standalone and counterfactual signals within a common branch update. Each counterfactual replaces one branch's choice and evaluates the resulting allocation from the actual pre-decision state.

\subsection{Constrained RL with Simplex Decomposition}
\label{sec:feasible-rcpo}

\paragraph{Sampling feasible allocations.}
We use the two-group map from Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} to enforce the action constraints in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid-problem}. Given observation $o_t$, the actor first samples a surrogate action $\bm u_t$. The environment then receives $\bm w_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)$. Write $\pi_\theta^u$ for the surrogate policy with parameters $\theta$ and $\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}$ for the induced allocation policy. Under $\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}$, the probability of an allocation set is the probability that $\mathsf T$ maps the sampled surrogate into that set. Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility} ensures that $\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ for every $\theta$ in the policy family. The resulting learning problem is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \max_\theta \quad &J_R(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
      &J_{C_q}(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})\le d_q,\qquad q=1,\ldots,Q.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:restricted-problem}
\end{equation}
The group shortfall $v(\bm w_t)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:group-shortfall} is zero on these allocations, so the penalty can focus on trajectory costs.

The actor uses an autoregressive policy. At decision $t$, it samples each trainable branch $i$ in order, conditioning on the observation $o_t$ and the sub-allocations $\bm y_{<i,t}=(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_{i-1,t})$ already determined for the preceding branches. Let $\mathcal I\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}$ be the fixed set of trainable branches. The joint density of the sampled components is
\begin{equation}
  \pi_\theta^u(\bm u_t\mid o_t)
    =\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}
       \pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t}).
  \label{eq:autoregressive-policy}
\end{equation}
A shared network encodes the observation, and each branch uses this encoding and the preceding sub-allocations. A Gaussian branch samples real-valued scores and converts them to shares with softmax. A Dirichlet branch samples the shares directly. For PPO, the conditional likelihood ratio compares the density of the same stored branch sample under the updated parameters $\theta$ and the rollout parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{i,t}(\theta)=
    \frac{\pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}},i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})},
  \qquad
  \rho_t(\theta)=\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}\rho_{i,t}(\theta).
  \label{eq:ppo-ratios}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_t$ is the joint ratio. For branch updates, we apply the clipped surrogate in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ppo-surrogate} to each conditional ratio.

\paragraph{Adapting the penalty to observed costs.}
The matched credit protocol uses one active trajectory cost, $C_1(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})$, with limit $d_1$. For each rollout, let $\widehat{\mathbb E}_t$ denote the empirical average over actual transitions and $\bar\xi=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t[C_1-d_1]$ their mean budget residual. After the actor and critic updates, we update the multiplier $\lambda$ once:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\leftarrow[\lambda+\eta_\lambda\bar\xi]_+,
  \qquad
  \eta_\lambda=
  \begin{cases}
    \eta_\uparrow,&\bar\xi>0,\\
    \eta_\downarrow,&\bar\xi\leq0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
The positive step sizes $\eta_\uparrow$ and $\eta_\downarrow$ control how quickly the penalty increases and decreases. Let $A_t^R$ and $A_t^C$ be the global GAE estimates from actual rewards and guiding costs, respectively. In the portfolio instance the guiding cost is $c_{t+1}$, and the residual used for the multiplier is $c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1}$. The advantages encode discounted feedback, while $\bar\xi$ uses the undiscounted budget criterion. The actor update thus approximates the constrained objective, and budget satisfaction must be assessed for the learned policy.

\paragraph{Shared and standalone credit.}
Shared credit gives each trainable branch the same actual reward and cost advantages $(A_t^R,A_t^C)$. Standalone credit instead scores the current sub-allocation $\bm y_{i,t}$ at full resource scale. It measures adjustment from the preceding sampled sub-allocation $\bm y_{i,t-1}$, initialised to the uniform allocation $\bm y_i^0$ on branch support $K_i$ at episode reset. The simulator stores these previous sub-allocations in modes that use standalone reward. The branch reward is computed directly on each transition and requires only this adjustment memory.

For example, in the portfolio environment, the standalone signal uses the same realised asset returns as the executed allocation:
\begin{equation}
  r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}=
  \log\!\left(\max\left\{\varepsilon_R,\,
    1+\bm y_{i,t}^{\top}\bm R_{t+1}
      -\kappa\lVert\bm y_{i,t}-\bm y_{i,t-1}\rVert_1\right\}\right).
  \label{eq:standalone-reward}
\end{equation}
Let $A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}$ be the GAE estimate from this reward. The standalone mode pairs it with $A_t^C$, so drawdown feedback still comes from the executed portfolio. Scoring a sub-allocation in isolation leaves out how its choice changes the combined allocation, including the mixing masses of other branches. This motivates a replacement comparison at the level of the executed action.

\subsection{Counterfactual Learning for Allocation Branches}
\label{sec:credit-analysis}

\paragraph{Constructing the comparison.}
For branch $i\in\mathcal I$, let $\bm y_i^0$ allocate equal shares to the entities in $K_i$ and zero elsewhere. At decision $t$ we construct a replacement allocation by substituting this reference into the same map,
\begin{equation}
  \bm w_t^{-i}
    =\CAOSD(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_i^0,\ldots,\bm y_{4,t}),
  \label{eq:counterfactual}
\end{equation}
where $-i$ identifies the branch being replaced. The actual allocation $\bm w_t$ uses all the sampled branches. Reapplying CAOSD recomputes the mixing coefficients and keeps $\bm w_t^{-i}$ in $\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}$. All other sub-allocations in the replacement are taken from the actual sample.

For a realised external transition from $x_t$ to $x_{t+1}$, let $F_t^f(h,\bm w)$ denote the one-step reward or guiding cost, indexed by $f$, evaluated at internal memory $h$ and allocation $\bm w$. Suppressing the common external inputs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-counterfactual-credit} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Delta f_{i,t}
    =F_t^f(h_t,\bm w_t)-F_t^f(h_t,\bm w_t^{-i}),
  \qquad f\in\{r,c\}.
  \label{eq:counterfactual-signals}
\end{equation}
Both evaluations start from the actual pre-decision memory $h_t$. Any internal state computed under $\bm w_t^{-i}$ serves only to score that transition. The real transition determines $h_{t+1}$ and the next observation.

\paragraph{Interpreting branch credit.}
The replacement can redistribute resources among branches whose choices were held fixed. For branch~2, let $z_{3,t}^{-2}$ be branch~3's recomputed share after replacement. The coefficient rules in Eq.~\eqref{eq:caosd-masses} give
\begin{equation}
  \bm w_t-\bm w_t^{-2}
    =z_{2,t}(\bm y_{2,t}-\bm y_2^0)
     +(z_{3,t}-z_{3,t}^{-2})(\bm y_{3,t}-\bm y_{4,t}).
  \label{eq:coupling-identity}
\end{equation}
The second term moves mass between branches~3 and~4 when the replacement changes the remaining requirement for the second group. This is why the entire allocation is recomputed.

Equation~\eqref{eq:counterfactual-signals} isolates the difference between the two current actions at the realised history. Identical mapped actions give zero difference in exact arithmetic. In the portfolio instance, both actions trade from $\bm w_{t-1}$, start from wealth $W_t$ and peak $M_t$, and use the same $\bm R_{t+1}$ and $b_{t+1}$. The replacement therefore changes the next net return and drawdown conditional on the actual account history. The shared allowance $\alpha_{t+1}$ cancels in the cost difference.

Later branches keep the samples drawn under the actual autoregressive prefix. Thus the comparison is conditional on those samples and describes a single transition. In particular, it does not estimate the effect of resampling later branches under the replacement or of following a different policy thereafter. A retained sample can depend on the replaced choice, so the reference does not in general give a gradient-preserving baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:baseline}).

\paragraph{Updating the branches.}
Let $A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}$ and $A_{i,t}^{\Delta C}$ denote GAE estimates from $\Delta r_{i,t}$ and $\Delta c_{i,t}$, respectively. The estimates use successive one-step signals on the actual rollout and bootstrap from actual observations. The matched modes use global and per-branch reward and cost heads, all receiving the same observation $o_t$. Whenever a mode uses a global signal, the corresponding global advantage and critic target are shared across branches. Table~\ref{tab:branch-credit} gives the combined advantage $A_{i,t}^{m}$ for each mode $m$.

For each branch we normalise the combined advantage across the rollout to obtain $\widetilde A_{i,t}^{m}$. The actor then maximises
\begin{equation}
  L_m(\theta)=
    \widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!
    \left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}
      \left(\Phi_\epsilon(\rho_{i,t}(\theta),\widetilde A_{i,t}^{m})
        +\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\,e_{i,t}(\theta)\right)\right],
  \label{eq:counterfactual-surrogate}
\end{equation}
where $e_{i,t}(\theta)$ is the conditional entropy of the branch distribution and $\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}$ is its regularisation coefficient. Summing over the fixed trainable set $\mathcal I$ gives the same unit weights for the clipped term and entropy in every matched mode. In particular, CF-C uses the unscaled standalone reward advantage. Within each reward or cost value loss, the global and branch mean-squared errors receive equal weight. Every mode updates the multiplier from the actual rollout residual by Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}.

\paragraph{Training procedure.}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:training} summarises the matched update. At evaluation, the actor and feasible map determine the allocation without computing branch credit. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} evaluates the five credit modes from Table~\ref{tab:branch-credit}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Training a feasible allocation policy with branch credit}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Credit mode $m$, initial actor and critics, multiplier $\lambda\geq0$
\While{the training budget remains}
  \State Reset the environment and, where used, initialise previous sub-allocations to $\bm y_i^0$.
  \State Copy $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\gets\theta$ and hold $\lambda$ fixed.
  \For{each transition in the rollout}
    \State Observe $o_t$, sample $\bm u_t$, and form $\bm w_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)$.
    \State Evaluate the actual transition and obtain its reward, guiding cost and budget residual.
    \State Where required, score standalone rewards using the previous sampled sub-allocations.
    \State For CF modes, score replacements from $h_t$ under the same external outcome.
    \State Advance the environment with the actual transition and retain branch weights for standalone rewards.
    \State Store observations, samples, log densities, selected signals, value predictions and termination flags.
    \State At episode end, reset the environment and any stored previous sub-allocations.
  \EndFor
  \State Compute global and branch critic targets and advantages using GAE on the actual rollout.
  \State Combine the advantages for mode $m$ and normalise them per branch.
  \State Update the actor and critics over PPO epochs with $\lambda$ fixed.
  \State Update $\lambda$ once from the actual rollout residual.
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setting and Metrics}

\paragraph{Training.}
The synthetic market has persistent volatility regimes, regime-dependent returns and correlations, and mild momentum. The actor observes trailing returns and account statistics as described in Section~\ref{sec:model}. For each training seed, the environment samples uniformly from eight market paths of 5,040 periods. Episodes last 252 steps, use a 20-period observation window, and start from the constrained-neutral allocation. The fee is one basis point per unit of turnover.

We train each learned method with seeds $\{0,1,2\}$ for 60,000 updates of 2,048 environment transitions, giving 122.88 million transitions per run. The networks have hidden layers of widths 192 and 128. We use minibatches of 512 and up to three PPO epochs per rollout, with a target KL divergence of $0.02$, clipping width $0.10$, and entropy coefficient $0.0015$. The Adam learning rate decreases linearly from $6\times10^{-5}$ to $3\times10^{-5}$. The discount and GAE trace decay are $0.99$ and $0.95$.

\paragraph{Comparisons.}
All learned actors are Gaussian. Alongside the static constrained-neutral benchmark, we include a softmax RCPO control and CAOSD with reward-only PPO. The softmax control uses one multiplier for the sum of squared relative group shortfalls divided by 20, plus $0.25$ times the drawdown cost. Its upward multiplier step size is $5\times10^{-4}$. The five CAOSD--RCPO modes share the conditional PPO surrogate, trainable-branch weights, critic inputs and multiplier update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}. Their reward and cost targets vary as specified in Table~\ref{tab:branch-credit}. Standalone serves as the reference for the matched credit comparison.

\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.}
We select one checkpoint per method and seed by maximising the mean difference in net cumulative return from constrained-neutral across 20 validation continuations from a fixed anchor. Validation runs after the first training update and every 200 updates. Each selected policy is evaluated deterministically on the same 100 held-out continuations, with 20 from each of five new anchors. For the learning curves, we evaluate a separate grid of 31 snapshots on the validation paths, starting at initialisation and then taking a snapshot every 2,000 updates.

\paragraph{Metrics and uncertainty.}
For each test path we compute terminal relative wealth $W_H/W_H^B-1$ and net cumulative return $W_H/W_0-1$. We also report maximum drawdown (MDD), including initial wealth in the running peak, and mean full-$\ell_1$ turnover. The mean cost gap is the average of $c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1}$ over the path. For every method, the path passes the common risk criterion if that gap is at most $10^{-10}$. We count steps with negative weights, a sum differing from one, or a group shortfall beyond tolerance $10^{-6}$. Matched differences are computed within each training seed and continuation. The 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals for test metrics resample training seeds and holdout anchor blocks, preserving the pairing. Intervals for the static benchmark use anchor blocks alone.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The distinction between isolated branch performance and contribution to the combined allocation is substantive even before comparing learning curves. Equation~\eqref{eq:coupling-identity} shows why a branch can affect mass outside its own term. The one-step comparison in Eq.~\eqref{eq:counterfactual-signals} holds past allocations and outcomes fixed, so its cost signal measures the change in the next outcome given the actual risk history. Whether this signal improves learning remains an empirical question.

The matched comparison holds the update form fixed, yet the cost advantage that drives a CF branch can point in a different direction from the actual portfolio cost that drives the multiplier. This distinction matters for CF-C, whose one-step cost credit coincides with a larger held-out cost gap than standalone. The experiment measures the performance of the resulting policies. Establishing which aspects of the credit signal drive that difference would require direct measurements of replacement magnitudes and how they enter the branch updates.

The portfolio results describe return-selected, deterministic Gaussian policies in one synthetic environment. The paired intervals reflect three training seeds and five holdout anchors. Budget satisfaction under stochastic execution or under checkpoint selection that prioritises cost would require a separate evaluation. Current drawdown cost and maximum drawdown capture different behaviour, since paths with similar maximum losses can spend different amounts of time above the drawdown threshold. Exogenous market returns make the replacement comparison available here. Applications with action-dependent external dynamics would need a transition model, and financial applications would require historical evaluation and more detailed execution accounting. Appendix~\ref{app:evidence} lists the remaining checks.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies branch credit in sequential constrained allocation with adjustment costs and path-dependent risk. An established CAOSD map enforces the simplex and two group limits, while an RCPO-inspired multiplier adapts the pressure on average path cost. Within this architecture, feasible neutral replacements furnish branch-specific reward and cost differences on individual transitions.

Each comparison evaluates both actions at the same realised history and captures redistribution through the allocation map. Retaining downstream autoregressive samples makes this comparison feasible to compute, but the resulting reference is not generally a gradient-preserving baseline.

In the matched portfolio comparison, all counterfactual wealth intervals relative to standalone include zero, and the cost-only variant increases the mean cost gap. The extra replacement evaluations also increase training time. These findings limit the evidence for one-step counterfactual credit in this setting, where all return-selected policies exceed the mean path-cost budget.

\section{Feasibility of the Two-Group CAOSD Map}
\label{app:feasibility}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:feasibility}]
For completeness, we verify the stated feasibility property of the CAOSD construction \citep{winkel2023}. Since $\ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1]$,
$0\leq z_1\leq\min\{\ell_1,\ell_2\}$ and $z_2=\ell_1-z_1\geq0$.
A non-empty second branch gives $q_2\in[0,1]$; an empty branch gives $q_2=0$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  0\leq z_3
     \leq\ell_2-z_1
     \leq1-\ell_1,\qquad
  z_4=1-\ell_1-z_3\geq0.
  \label{eq:proof-mass-bound}
\end{equation}
The upper bound uses
$\ell_2-z_1=\min\{\ell_2,1-\ell_1\}$.
The coefficients sum to one by definition.

It remains to check that an empty branch cannot remove positive mass from the allocation. Non-emptiness of the feasible set excludes $V_k=\varnothing$ whenever $\ell_k>0$. Thus $K_2=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_1=z_1=z_2=0$, while $K_3=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_2=z_1=z_3=0$. If $K_1=\varnothing$, the groups are disjoint; feasibility then requires $\ell_1+\ell_2\leq1$, giving $z_1=0$. Since $K_4=\mathcal E$ is non-empty, every positive coefficient multiplies a non-negative unit-mass branch. Branches with zero coefficients contribute nothing, regardless of whether their supports are empty. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf T(\bm u)\geq\bm0,\qquad
  \mathbf1^\top\mathsf T(\bm u)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i=1.
  \label{eq:proof-simplex}
\end{equation}
The prescribed supports further imply
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in V_1}w_j\geq z_1+z_2=\ell_1,\qquad
  \sum_{j\in V_2}w_j\geq z_1+z_2q_2+z_3\geq\ell_2.
  \label{eq:proof-exposure}
\end{equation}
All omitted contributions to either group are non-negative. This proves simplex membership and both exposure bounds, including overlapping groups, disjoint groups and zero-mass branches.\end{proof}

In floating-point arithmetic, the implementation clips negligible negative round-off and normalises the final allocation. Numerical feasibility diagnostics are interpreted relative to their tolerance.

\section{Portfolio Parameters and Numerical Conventions}
\label{app:instance}

\subsection{Instance parameters}

With the simulator's zero-based entity identifiers, the experimental groups and minimum exposures are
\begin{equation}
  V_1=\{1,4,6\},\quad \ell_1=0.50;\qquad
  V_2=\{2,4,7\},\quad \ell_2=0.40.
  \label{eq:portfolio-groups}
\end{equation}
These identifiers describe the instance without fixing $N$ in the general formulation. Since $\ell_1+\ell_2<1$, the first coefficient is identically zero and branch~1 receives no policy update. Branches~2--4 remain trainable; the overlap still influences $q_2$ and $z_3$. Agent and benchmark start at the CAOSD image of uniform branch weights. The benchmark retains this target and incurs neither an initial target-change fee nor subsequent target-change fees under the simulator's accounting convention.

The parameters in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth}--\eqref{eq:portfolio-alpha} and \eqref{eq:lambda} are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    \kappa=10^{-4},\qquad b_{\min}=0.05,\qquad
    \beta=0.90,\qquad s_C=0.10,\qquad \nu=0.05,\\
    \varepsilon_R=10^{-6},\qquad \varepsilon_W=10^{-12},\qquad
    \eta_\uparrow=0.00075,\qquad\eta_\downarrow=0.01,\qquad\lambda_0=0.
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:instance-parameters}
\end{equation}
The benchmark adapts the drawdown threshold and hence the cost allowance. The multiplier responds once per rollout to the realised mean residual; neither mechanism projects the portfolio onto a path-risk-feasible set. Current drawdown determines the training cost, while maximum drawdown is retained as a diagnostic and evaluation statistic.

\subsection{Accounting and numerical safeguards}

For two probability vectors,
$T_t\leq\lVert\bm w_t\rVert_1+\lVert\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1=2$.
Moreover, the portfolio's gross return is a convex combination of entity gross returns. Hence
\begin{equation}
  g_{t+1}\geq
     \min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})-2\kappa.
  \label{eq:positive-growth-bound}
\end{equation}
The condition $\min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})>2\kappa$ is sufficient for positive net growth. It is not implied by simplex membership, and the Gaussian return model does not guarantee it. Numerical floors keep individual updates defined, but do not model liquidation, default or an economically self-financing recovery. If either floor is active, reward need not equal the log wealth increment. In exact arithmetic, positive floored growth and $W_0=W_0^B=1$ keep both running peaks at least one and drawdown denominators non-zero; this does not rule out floating-point underflow over arbitrary horizons.

Exact holdings-level self-financing would require measuring trades from return-drifted pre-trade holdings and accounting for fee payment when determining post-trade wealth and weights \citep{jiang2017}. Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth} instead deducts a proportional target-change proxy. Full-vector turnover includes reserve transfers, but slippage, tiered fees and market impact are absent. A constant-target benchmark is also not charged for rebalancing caused by holding drift. The empirical results must be read under these accounting assumptions.

For numerical PPO evaluation, joint and conditional log-probability differences are clipped to $[-20,20]$ before exponentiation. Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-ratios} gives the unmodified ratios, recovered when this safeguard is inactive. For any combined advantage vector $A$ over a rollout, normalisation is
$\widetilde A=(A-\operatorname{mean}(A))/(\operatorname{sd}(A)+10^{-8})$,
where $\operatorname{sd}$ is the population standard deviation. Branches are normalised separately after combining reward and cost. Each rollout starts with an environment reset. Within a rollout, actual and benchmark account statistics and, where needed, previous sub-allocations are reset at each new episode.

\subsection{Policy independence of the benchmark allowance}

Let $\mathbb P_x$ denote the law of the exogenous market trajectory. Market sampling, horizon $H$, benchmark target and initial benchmark state are fixed independently of $\theta$. Its allowance is therefore a functional $\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})$ of that trajectory. For every admissible allocation policy,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]
      &=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_x}\!\left[
        \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})\right],\\
    \nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]&=\bm0.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:allowance-independence}
\end{equation}
The expectation is constant in $\theta$, so no interchange of differentiation and integration is needed. Fixed discount weights give the same policy-independence property. This explains why the global critic can omit the allowance from its policy-dependent cost target. It does not remove the actor--dual discount mismatch or make the branch objectives exact Lagrangian gradients.

\section{A Retained Downstream Sample Need Not Be a Valid Baseline}
\label{app:baseline}

A scalar example isolates the dependence introduced by autoregressive sampling. It is an analytical illustration, not an additional portfolio experiment. Let a first variable $U_1$ and a conditional second variable $U_2$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  U_1\sim\mathcal N(\theta,1),\qquad
  U_2\mid U_1\sim\mathcal N(U_1,1),\qquad
  F(U_1,U_2)=U_1+U_2.
  \label{eq:baseline-example}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, $\theta$ is a scalar policy parameter, and $F$ is the outcome. The conditional kernel of $U_2$ has no explicit dependence on $\theta$ when $U_1$ is held fixed.

The expected outcome is $2\theta$, so its derivative is $2$. Replacing $U_1$ by zero while retaining realised $U_2$ gives the baseline $F(0,U_2)=U_2$ and difference $U_1$. The first-variable log-density derivative is $U_1-\theta$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E[(U_1-\theta)F(U_1,U_2)]=2,\qquad
  \mathbb E[(U_1-\theta)\{F(U_1,U_2)-F(0,U_2)\}]=1.
  \label{eq:baseline-counterexample}
\end{equation}
The baseline term has non-zero score expectation because $U_2$ depends on $U_1$. A fixed downstream sample therefore cannot generally be treated as an action-independent baseline for an earlier autoregressive decision. This example does not imply that difference credit cannot be useful; it shows why its learning effect needs evidence rather than an automatic unbiasedness claim.

\begin{enumerate}
