\begin{abstract}
Sequential resource allocation requires policies that balance utility, adjustment costs and path-dependent risk while respecting constraints on every action. This report studies the integration of established reinforcement learning (RL) methods for these distinct requirements. Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition (CAOSD) constructs allocations satisfying the simplex and two possibly overlapping group-exposure constraints. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) learns an autoregressive policy over the decomposed action space, and a multiplier update inspired by Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) adapts the pressure on trajectory cost. A portfolio instance specifies the information available at each decision, target-change transaction costs and a benchmark-referenced drawdown allowance. Within this architecture, standalone branch credit is compared with open-loop counterfactual credit based on feasible neutral-replacement paths. Evaluation on 20 shared unseen synthetic continuations shows that credit-mode rankings depend on the actor distribution and performance criterion. Counterfactual-reward Dirichlet RCPO achieves the highest observed mean relative wealth, but its advantage over the standalone control is inconclusive and turnover increases. Allocation feasibility is preserved, whereas the path-risk target remains unsatisfied on the pooled evaluation statistic. The study contributes an explicit formulation, implementation-level integration and empirical comparison, with evidence conditional on one trained seed and a synthetic market distribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Contributions}

Sequential allocation distributes a finite resource among competing entities over time. Its value depends on both the allocation selected and the sequence of decisions that precedes it: reallocating resources can incur adjustment costs, while accumulated outcomes determine exposure to path-dependent risk. These features arise in capital allocation, workload scheduling, logistics and energy management. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a framework for learning the resulting intertemporal trade-offs.

Two requirements govern such decisions. \emph{Hard action constraints} specify admissible allocations through non-negativity, resource conservation and group exposures. \emph{Cumulative cost constraints} regulate outcomes generated by a sequence of decisions. A valid allocation can still increase path risk, and a small average penalty need not exclude inadmissible actions. Portfolio allocation makes this distinction concrete: exposure limits constrain weights, transaction costs depend on changes in allocation, and drawdown depends on the wealth history. The execution model and information available to the investor are therefore part of the control problem \cite{jiang2017,gollart2025}.

\subsection{Related work and scope}

Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) augment a reward objective with expected cumulative-cost limits. Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) derives updates from bounds on changes in expected returns and costs \cite{achiam2017}, while Reward Constrained Policy Optimization (RCPO) learns a penalty multiplier on a slower timescale than the policy \cite{tessler2019}. These methods address expected outcomes, one of several distinct notions of safety in RL \cite{kushwaha2025}. Action-constrained RL instead builds admissibility into action selection or policy optimisation \cite{lin2021}.

For allocation problems, Constrained Allocation Optimization with Simplex Decomposition (CAOSD) constructs feasible actions from coupled simplex-valued sub-actions under two group constraints \cite{winkel2023}. Polytope Action Space Policy Optimization (PASPO) treats more general linearly constrained allocation spaces \cite{winkel2024paspo}. This report uses the two-group CAOSD construction. The separation of known instantaneous structure from learned intertemporal decisions also appears in Ding et al.\ \cite{ding2021}; here, the structural component constructs admissible actions, while RL optimises their sequential consequences.

The question studied is how a structurally feasible allocation policy can be coupled to adaptive path-cost learning, and how branch-level credit affects the resulting behaviour. The architecture combines CAOSD, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017} and an RCPO-inspired update. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A constrained-allocation formulation that separates instantaneous feasibility from cumulative cost and specifies the information and memory needed for path-dependent control.
  \item An explicit integration of a two-group feasible policy, PPO learning and adaptive risk penalties, including the branch objectives and multiplier-update schedule.
  \item A portfolio comparison of standalone and open-loop counterfactual credit under shared evaluation paths, examining return, risk, turnover and constraint satisfaction.
\end{enumerate}
The general contribution concerns the formulation and learning interface; empirical evidence comes from the portfolio instance. The component methods retain their original attribution. Credit-mode comparisons assess the implemented training schemes, whose weighting and regularisation also differ. Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:learning} develop the model, preliminaries and learning procedure before the experimental evaluation.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:model}

We consider sequential allocation of a divisible resource among $N$ entities. A decision must satisfy the allocation requirements at the time it is made, while its value depends on subsequent outcomes and the cost of changing earlier allocations. The portfolio environment provides a concrete specification of these dynamics and of the path-risk constraint.

\subsection{Allocation system}

The decision horizon consists of $H$ periods indexed by $t=0,\ldots,H-1$. Let $\mathcal E=\{0,\ldots,N-1\}$ denote the entity set and let $a_{t,i}$ be the resource share assigned to entity $i$. Complete allocation with non-negative shares requires
\begin{equation}
  \bm a_t\in\mathcal A=\AllocSimplex,\qquad
  \AllocSimplex
  :=\{\bm a\in\R^N:a_i\geq0\ \text{for all }i\in\mathcal E,\
                     \mathbf1^\top\bm a=1\}.
  \label{eq:allocation-simplex}
\end{equation}
Here $N$ counts coordinates. For two prescribed groups $V_1,V_2\subseteq\mathcal E$ and minimum shares $\ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1]$, the admissible action set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal A_{\mathrm h}
  =\left\{\bm a\in\AllocSimplex:
       \sum_{i\in V_k}a_i\geq\ell_k,\quad k=1,2\right\}.
  \label{eq:hard-action-set}
\end{equation}
The groups may overlap, and their limits are assumed to admit at least one feasible allocation. An upper limit $\sum_{i\in V}a_i\leq u$ can be written as the lower limit $\sum_{i\in\mathcal E\setminus V}a_i\geq1-u$. The two-group form in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hard-action-set} is the geometry treated by the implemented CAOSD map \cite{winkel2023}. General linearly constrained allocation spaces require a broader construction, such as PASPO \cite{winkel2024paspo}. The groups and thresholds used in the portfolio experiment are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:instance}.

\subsection{Portfolio state evolution}

In the portfolio instance, $\bm w_t=\bm a_t$ is the target weight vector and $\bm R_{t+1}\in\R^N$ is the vector of simple asset returns over the next period. A zero-return reserve occupies one of the same $N$ coordinates. Returns are exogenous to the allocation. The investor selects $\bm w_t$ using information available at time $t$, after which $\bm R_{t+1}$ is realised and the account is updated.

Let $\kappa\geq0$ be the proportional transaction-cost coefficient. The simulator measures turnover from the previous target and defines net growth as
\begin{equation}
  T_t=\lVert\bm w_t-\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1,\qquad
  g_{t+1}=1+\bm w_t^\top\bm R_{t+1}-\kappa T_t.
  \label{eq:portfolio-net-growth}
\end{equation}
The full weight vector enters $T_t$, including reallocations to and from the reserve. With initial wealth $W_0=1$, the wealth process and reward are
\begin{equation}
  W_{t+1}=W_t\max\{\varepsilon_W,g_{t+1}\},\qquad
  r_t=\log\!\bigl(\max\{\varepsilon_R,g_{t+1}\}\bigr),
  \label{eq:portfolio-reward}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_W,\varepsilon_R>0$ are the numerical floors used by the implementation. When neither is active, $r_t=\log W_{t+1}-\log W_t$. The initial target $\bm w_{-1}$ is the constrained-neutral allocation, with previous-turnover memory $T_{-1}=0$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth} is a target-change cost model. Exact self-financing accounting would instead measure trades from return-drifted holdings and account for fee payment in the post-trade capital and weights \cite{jiang2017}. The simulator uses the target-change approximation throughout. Appendix~\ref{app:instance} records the numerical constants, omitted execution frictions and positive-growth condition.

\subsection{Benchmark-referenced path risk}

Risk is measured from the realised wealth history. Define the running peak, current drawdown and maximum drawdown by
\begin{equation}
  M_t=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}W_v,\qquad
  D_t=1-\frac{W_t}{M_t},\qquad
  D_t^{\max}=\max_{0\leq v\leq t}D_v.
  \label{eq:portfolio-drawdown}
\end{equation}
A constrained-neutral benchmark follows a fixed feasible target on the same return path. Superscript $B$ denotes its corresponding quantities, with $W_0^B=M_0^B=1$. Both wealth peaks include initial wealth.

For parameters $b_{\min}>0$, $\beta>0$ and $s_C>0$, the drawdown threshold and transition cost are
\begin{equation}
  b_{t+1}=\max\{b_{\min},\beta D_{t+1}^B\},\qquad
  c_{t+1}=\frac{[D_{t+1}-b_{t+1}]_+^2}{s_C},
  \label{eq:portfolio-cost}
\end{equation}
where $[x]_+=\max(0,x)$. Thus the charged cost depends on current drawdown, whereas $D_t^{\max}$ records the largest drawdown for diagnosis and evaluation. A separate cost allowance is defined by
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{t+1}=\frac{(\nu b_{t+1})^2}{s_C},\qquad \nu\geq0.
  \label{eq:portfolio-alpha}
\end{equation}
The threshold $b_{t+1}$ has drawdown units; $\alpha_{t+1}$ is the permitted cost level and is compared with $c_{t+1}$. Both respond to the benchmark path rather than to a fixed drawdown ceiling. At decision $t$, the available threshold is $b_t$. The next return determines $b_{t+1}$ and the cost of that completed transition. This ordering makes risk accounting causal without exposing future returns to the policy.

\subsection{Information structure and policy objective}

A state description must retain the memory needed by the preceding updates. Let $x_t$ be a sufficient state of the exogenous environment, including any history needed to construct market features, and write
\begin{equation}
  s_t=(x_t,\bm a_{t-1},\zeta_t,t),\qquad
  s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid s_t,\bm a_t).
  \label{eq:augmented-state}
\end{equation}
The vector $\zeta_t$ collects decision-dependent system and path statistics. For the portfolio environment it contains
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_t=(W_t,M_t,D_t^{\max},
           W_t^B,M_t^B,D_t^{B,\max},T_{t-1}).
  \label{eq:portfolio-path-state}
\end{equation}
Previous allocation is essential: identical market conditions and identical new targets can produce different transaction costs from different previous targets. Wealth and peak memory similarly determine the subsequent drawdown cost.

The actor observes $o_t=\mathsf O(s_t)$, consisting of trailing returns, rolling means and volatility, previous target weights and turnover, allocation diagnostics, current and maximum drawdowns of agent and benchmark, the current threshold and drawdown gap, and elapsed time. It receives neither latent regime labels nor absolute wealth. The Markov model is defined on $s_t$; the finite observation window is not assumed to identify that full state. The implemented feedforward policy consequently operates under partial observation.

These dynamics define a finite-horizon CMDP \cite{achiam2017},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,R,\gamma,\mathcal C),
  \qquad \mathcal C=(C_1,\ldots,C_Q),
  \label{eq:allocation-mdp}
\end{equation}
with initial-state distribution $\rho_0$. The reward kernel $R(s_t,\bm a_t,s_{t+1})=r_t$ is distinct from the asset-return vector $\bm R_{t+1}$, and $C_q$ are transition-cost functions. Reward and cost returns are assumed integrable under the policies considered. Let $\mathcal F_t$ contain the observations, past actions and realised feedback available before decision $t$. A policy $\pi_t(\cdot\mid\mathcal F_t)$ is non-anticipative. Define $\Pi_{\mathrm h}$ as the class of such policies satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \pi_t(\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}\mid\mathcal F_t)=1
  \quad\text{almost surely},\qquad t=0,\ldots,H-1.
  \label{eq:hard-feasibility}
\end{equation}
This support requirement applies to every executed action, irrespective of the cumulative cost.

For $\gamma\in(0,1]$, define the reward return and expected average costs as
\begin{align}
  J_R(\pi)
    &=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
        \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^t r_t\right],
  \label{eq:reward-return}\\
  J_{C_q}(\pi)
    &=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[
        \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}C_q(s_t,\bm a_t,s_{t+1})\right].
  \label{eq:cost-return}
\end{align}
Undiscounted average costs express the finite-horizon risk requirement, while $\gamma$ discounts the reward. Given cost limits $d_q$, the policy problem is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}}\quad &J_R(\pi)\\
    \text{subject to}\quad
      &J_{C_q}(\pi)\leq d_q,\qquad q=1,\ldots,Q.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hybrid-problem}
\end{equation}
For the portfolio risk requirement, take one constraint with centred cost
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    C_1(s_t,\bm w_t,s_{t+1})
      &=c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1},\qquad d_1=0,\\
    J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi)
      &=\mathbb E_\pi\!\left[\frac1H
          \sum_{t=0}^{H-1}(c_{t+1}-\alpha_{t+1})\right]\leq0.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:adaptive-risk-problem}
\end{equation}
The random allowance remains inside the expectation. This condition limits expected average residual cost, while Eq.~\eqref{eq:hard-feasibility} imposes instantaneous allocation feasibility. The policy update uses discounted cost advantages as a guiding penalty for this average-cost target; Section~\ref{sec:learning} specifies the approximation. Maximum drawdown and terminal relative wealth $W_H/W_H^B-1$ are evaluation statistics for the portfolio instance.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}
\label{sec:reformulation}

Two existing ideas provide the basis of the learning architecture: a feasible allocation map and a penalty-based policy update. Their roles are introduced here before specifying the implemented policy and credit schemes.

\subsection{Two-group simplex decomposition}

Rather than sampling an environment allocation directly, the learner samples surrogate variables $\bm u_t$ and applies a deterministic map:
\begin{equation}
  \bm a_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)\in\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}.
  \label{eq:surrogate-map}
\end{equation}
The environment evaluates its transition, reward and cost using the mapped action. For the CAOSD construction \cite{winkel2023}, specialise $\mathcal A_{\mathrm h}$ to two possibly overlapping group lower bounds:

\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in V_1}a_j\geq\ell_1,\qquad
  \sum_{j\in V_2}a_j\geq\ell_2,\qquad \ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1].
  \label{eq:two-group-constraints}
\end{equation}
The four branch supports are
\begin{equation}
  K_1=V_1\cap V_2,\quad K_2=V_1,\quad
  K_3=V_2,\quad K_4=\mathcal E.
  \label{eq:caosd-branches}
\end{equation}
Each surrogate component $\bm u_i$ determines a padded vector $\bm y_i\in\R_+^N$, with zero coordinates outside $K_i$ and unit mass within a non-empty support. For an empty support, set $\bm y_i=\bm0$. Define the overlap share of branch~2 as
\begin{equation}
  q_2=\sum_{j\in V_1\cap V_2}y_{2,j}.
  \label{eq:q2}
\end{equation}
Define the mixing coefficients and final allocation by
\begin{align}
  z_1&=[\ell_1+\ell_2-1]_+,&
  z_2&=[\ell_1-z_1]_+,\nonumber\\
  z_3&=[\ell_2-z_1-z_2q_2]_+,&
  z_4&=1-z_1-z_2-z_3,
  \label{eq:caosd-masses}\\
  \mathsf T(\bm u)
    &=\CAOSD(\bm y_1,\ldots,\bm y_4)
      =\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i\bm y_i.
  \label{eq:caosd}
\end{align}
Branch~1 supplies mandatory overlap mass, branch~2 completes the first lower bound, branch~3 supplies any remaining second-group requirement, and branch~4 distributes the residual. Thus the share $q_2$ couples branch~2 to the masses assigned to later branches. Time subscripts identify the coefficients induced by the sampled branches at decision $t$.

\paragraph{Proposition 1 (two-group CAOSD feasibility \cite{winkel2023}).}
Suppose the two-group feasible set is non-empty and each non-empty branch is a probability vector on its prescribed support. Then $z_i\geq0$ and $\sum_i z_i=1$; every empty branch has zero coefficient; and $\mathsf T(\bm u)$ satisfies Eqs.~\eqref{eq:allocation-simplex} and \eqref{eq:two-group-constraints}. This includes disjoint groups and vanishing branch coefficients. Appendix~\ref{app:feasibility} gives the proof.

Feasibility is an exact-arithmetic property of this map and does not depend on critic accuracy or multiplier convergence. Numerical checks use floating-point tolerances. Appendix~\ref{app:instance} gives the portfolio groups.

\subsection{Lagrangian risk learning and PPO}

For one constraint in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hybrid-problem}, write its cost return and budget as $J_C$ and $d_C$. The reward-maximisation Lagrangian is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)
    =J_R(\pi)-\lambda\bigl(J_C(\pi)-d_C\bigr),
  \qquad \lambda\geq0.
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
Primal policy improvement favours reward while penalising cost; dual adjustment raises the multiplier when the estimated constraint residual is positive. RCPO separates these updates across timescales and permits a discounted guiding penalty for policy learning \cite{tessler2019}. Its convergence analysis depends on assumptions about the penalty, optimisation and update schedules.

PPO uses a clipped likelihood-ratio objective for updates on stored rollouts \cite{schulman2017}. For likelihood ratio $\rho$, advantage estimate $A$ and clipping parameter $0<\epsilon<1$, define
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_\epsilon(\rho,A)
    =\min\{\rho A,\operatorname{clip}(\rho,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A\}.
  \label{eq:ppo-surrogate}
\end{equation}
Reward and cost critics supply baselines for generalised advantage estimation (GAE). All symbols $A$ below denote estimates, rather than exact advantages. The application of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ppo-surrogate} to a joint surrogate action or to individual branches determines different learning objectives.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:learning}

The actor samples decomposed surrogate actions, CAOSD maps them to a feasible allocation, and the realised reward and cost train the actor and critics. A global multiplier adapts to actual path cost. Branch credit changes the policy-learning signals while leaving the executed action map and dual cost statistic unchanged.

\subsection{Feasible autoregressive multi-branch policy}

Let $\pi_\theta^u$ denote the surrogate actor. Its induced allocation law is the pushforward $\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}=\mathsf T_\#\pi_\theta^u$: the probability of an allocation set is the probability that the sampled surrogate maps into that set. The portfolio problem within this family is
\begin{equation}
  \max_\theta J_R(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})
  \quad\text{subject to}\quad
  J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})\leq0,
  \qquad \pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}\in\Pi_{\mathrm h}.
  \label{eq:restricted-problem}
\end{equation}
The map enforces the hard condition throughout learning. The neural family and its optimiser determine which feasible policies are learned; map feasibility alone establishes neither coverage of all admissible policies nor global optimality. The neural implementation additionally requires all four supports $K_i$ to be non-empty, even though Proposition~1 permits empty supports with zero mass.

Let $\mathcal I\subseteq\{1,2,3,4\}$ be the fixed implementation mask of trainable branches, and let
$\bm y_{<i,t}=(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_{i-1,t})$ denote preceding branch representations. The surrogate actor factorises as
\begin{equation}
  \pi_\theta^u(\bm u_t\mid o_t)
     =\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}
       \pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t}),
  \qquad
  \log\pi_\theta^u=\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}\log\pi_{\theta,i}.
  \label{eq:autoregressive-policy}
\end{equation}
A shared multilayer perceptron encodes $o_t$, and successive heads condition on preceding realised branch weights. Gaussian heads sample logits followed by branch softmax; Dirichlet heads sample weights using bounded positive concentrations. Masked heads use deterministic neutral values and are omitted from the likelihood. In the reported instance, $\mathcal I=\{2,3,4\}$. A temporarily zero mixing coefficient does not itself remove a head from this fixed mask. Likelihoods are evaluated in surrogate coordinates, so no density of the generally many-to-one mapped allocation is required.

For rollout parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$, the conditional and joint likelihood ratios are
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{i,t}(\theta)=
    \frac{\pi_{\theta,i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}},i}(\bm u_{i,t}\mid o_t,\bm y_{<i,t})},
  \qquad
  \rho_t(\theta)=\prod_{i\in\mathcal I}\rho_{i,t}(\theta).
  \label{eq:ppo-ratios}
\end{equation}
Both probabilities condition on the predecessor values stored in the rollout. A joint PPO update uses $\rho_t$, whereas the branch objectives below use $\rho_{i,t}$. This distinction is essential when attributing a shared outcome to dependent branch decisions.

\subsection{Standalone branch learning and virtual-agent interpretation}

The executed allocation produces one reward and one path cost. The standalone control instead assigns branch~$i$ a reward from an auxiliary portfolio invested fully according to $\bm y_{i,t}$, with its own previous weights, wealth and risk history. The corresponding estimate $A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}$ describes isolated branch performance. It is combined with the actual portfolio's cost advantage, so all branches face the same global risk multiplier.

This admits a virtual-agent interpretation: each branch selects a sub-allocation and receives a learning signal, while the feasible map combines their decisions. The branches share parameters and act sequentially within one environment decision. They are therefore modules of a single autoregressive policy, rather than independently acting agents in a separate Markov game. Auxiliary standalone allocations need not satisfy both group bounds and are never executed. Their rewards are useful training targets, but are not exact marginal contributions to the final portfolio.

\subsection{Open-loop counterfactual branch credit}

Counterfactual (CF) credit instead replaces one branch by the uniform neutral representation $\bm y_i^0$ on its support, while preserving all other realised branches:
\begin{align}
  \bm a_t^{\mathrm{act}}&=\CAOSD(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_{4,t}),\nonumber\\
  \bm a_t^{-i}&=\CAOSD(\bm y_{1,t},\ldots,\bm y_i^0,\ldots,\bm y_{4,t}).
  \label{eq:counterfactual}
\end{align}
Both allocations use the feasible map. Recomputing it is essential because replacing branch~2 can change $q_2$ and the downstream masses. On the same realised market outcome, define
\begin{equation}
  \Delta r_{i,t}=r_t^{\mathrm{act}}-r_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cf}},\qquad
  \Delta c_{i,t+1}=c_{t+1}^{\mathrm{act}}-c_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{cf}}.
  \label{eq:counterfactual-signals}
\end{equation}
Each neutral-replacement path retains its own previous weights, turnover, wealth, peak and drawdown. Its critics receive the corresponding path features. Actual and shadow paths share market returns and the benchmark allowance, so the allowance cancels in their residual-cost difference. A positive $\Delta c_{i,t+1}$ means the actual path incurs more cost than that shadow path. Because the histories persist, the contrast includes accumulated effects of earlier replacements as well as the current allocation.

The construction draws on counterfactual credit assignment \cite{foerster2018}. It uses a fixed neutral replacement, rather than marginalising a branch action under its policy. Later autoregressive branches retain their realised values after substitution, making the comparison open-loop. The signal is consequently a path-consistent credit heuristic, not an unbiased one-step causal effect or a closed-loop policy contrast. Its implementation exploits exogenous market returns and explicit accounting updates; tasks with action-dependent external dynamics would require an appropriate counterfactual transition model.

\subsection{Policy objectives, regularisation and adaptive risk learning}

For the portfolio constraint, write the Lagrangian relaxation as
$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ad}}(\theta,\lambda)
=J_R(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})-\lambda J_{\mathrm{gap}}(\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h})$.
The actor uses discounted reward and cost advantage estimates. In a global update,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    A_t^{\mathrm{global}}&=A_t^R-\lambda A_t^C,\\
    L_{\mathrm{global}}(\theta)
      &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t[
         \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_t,\widetilde A_t^{\mathrm{global}})],
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:rcpo-advantage}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathbb E}_t$ is an empirical rollout average and a tilde denotes normalisation. Under the fixed-horizon, exogenous-market protocol, the expected benchmark allowance is policy-independent. The global cost critic therefore estimates $c$, rather than $c-\alpha$; Appendix~\ref{app:instance} gives the derivative argument.

Let $A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}$ and $A_{i,t}^{\Delta C}$ be GAE estimates for the difference signals in Eq.~\eqref{eq:counterfactual-signals}. The standalone control and three counterfactual variants use
\begin{align}
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}&=A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}-\lambda A_t^C,\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-R}}&=A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}-\lambda A_t^C,\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-C}}&=z_{i,t}A_{i,t}^{R,\mathrm{st}}-\lambda A_{i,t}^{\Delta C},\nonumber\\
  A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF-RC}}&=A_{i,t}^{\Delta R}-\lambda A_{i,t}^{\Delta C}.
  \label{eq:branch-advantages}
\end{align}
CF-R pairs counterfactual reward with global cost; CF-C pairs standalone reward with counterfactual cost; CF-RC uses both differences. Each combined advantage is normalised separately per branch \emph{after} reward--cost combination. Denote the result by $\widetilde A_{i,t}^{m}$ for mode $m$, and let $\chi_i=\mathbf1\{i\in\mathcal I\}$. Setting $\rho_{i,t}=1$ for masked branches, the objectives are
\begin{align}
  L_{\mathrm{st}}(\theta)
    &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!
       \left[\sum_{i\in\mathcal I}z_{i,t}
         \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_{i,t},\widetilde A_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}})\right],
       \label{eq:standalone-surrogate}\\
  L_{\mathrm{CF},m}(\theta)
    &=\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!
       \left[\sum_{i=1}^{4}\chi_i
         \Phi_\epsilon(\rho_{i,t},\widetilde A_{i,t}^{m})\right],
       \quad m\in\{\mathrm{CF\!-\!R},\mathrm{CF\!-\!C},\mathrm{CF\!-\!RC}\}.
       \label{eq:counterfactual-surrogate}
\end{align}
Recorded masses and masks are fixed during optimisation. Standalone mass weights multiply the entire clipped surrogate, including its cost contribution. The counterfactual objective uses the mask, without a second mass factor on portfolio-level differences. In CF-C, the standalone reward is mass-scaled before combination and normalisation; the cost difference is not. These are implemented surrogate choices, not algebraically equivalent decompositions of $L_{\mathrm{global}}$.

Entropy regularisation follows the same external weighting convention. With $h_{i,t}(\theta)$ the conditional differential entropy of branch~$i$ in its sampled surrogate coordinates, the bonus is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal H_m(\theta)
    =\widehat{\mathbb E}_t\!\left[
       \sum_{i=1}^{4}\omega_{i,t}^{m}h_{i,t}(\theta)\right],
  \qquad
  \omega_{i,t}^{\mathrm{st}}=z_{i,t},\quad
  \omega_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CF}}=\chi_i.
  \label{eq:branch-entropy}
\end{equation}
The actor maximises $L_m+\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\mathcal H_m$, with entropy coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{ent}}\geq0$, while critics regress to the returns of their selected signals. Equal entropy coefficients therefore do not imply identical regularisation across credit modes. Together with the mass weighting and critic inputs, this makes the reported comparison a comparison of training schemes rather than an isolated intervention on the credit signal.

The multiplier is held fixed throughout each rollout's PPO epochs. After the actor--critic update it changes once, using actual rollout means:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\leftarrow[\lambda+\eta_\lambda(\bar c-\bar\alpha)]_+,\qquad
  \eta_\lambda=
  \begin{cases}
    \eta_\uparrow,&\bar c>\bar\alpha,\\
    \eta_\downarrow,&\bar c\leq\bar\alpha.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
An excess raises the penalty; a negative residual relaxes it subject to non-negativity. Every mode uses actual non-negative cost for this update, including modes whose branch critics estimate signed differences. PPO controls set $\lambda=0$ and omit the dual update.

This RCPO-inspired procedure uses discounted GAE for the actor and an undiscounted rollout mean for the dual statistic. Finite sampling, advantage normalisation and the modified branch surrogates further separate it from exact optimisation of $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ad}}$. The resulting adaptive penalty is an approximate learning mechanism; the original RCPO convergence result does not establish feasibility of this implementation or of each realised risk path.

\medskip
\textbf{Algorithm 1: Feasible allocation learning with branch credit}
\begin{enumerate}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{0.15em}
  \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item Initialise actor, critics, $\lambda\geq0$, and actual, benchmark and shadow-path memories.
  \item Repeat for each rollout:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \setlength{\itemsep}{0.1em}
      \setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
      \item Fix rollout parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$ and the current multiplier $\lambda$.
      \item At each decision, observe $o_t$, sample branches autoregressively, and execute $\bm a_t=\mathsf T(\bm u_t)$. On the realised market outcome, update actual and benchmark paths, compute $b_{t+1},c_{t+1},\alpha_{t+1}$, and update the required shadow paths.
      \item Store observations, surrogate actions, conditional log probabilities, masses, critic values and selected signals. Reset all path memories at episode boundaries only.
      \item Bootstrap unfinished trajectories, compute reward and cost GAE, combine advantages according to the credit mode, and normalise them.
      \item With $\lambda$ fixed, update the actor and critics over PPO epochs and minibatches using the applicable global or branch objective.
      \item For RCPO, update $\lambda$ once from the actual rollout residual $\bar c-\bar\alpha$ using Eq.~\eqref{eq:lambda}. Carry ongoing path memories into the next rollout.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Protocol and metrics}

The synthetic generator contains persistent low- and high-volatility regimes, regime-dependent relative winners, mild momentum, and regime-dependent correlations. Assets favoured in calm markets differ from the defensive assets favoured in volatile regimes, forcing the policy to infer the latent state from observations rather than receive a regime label. Training samples uniformly from eight deterministic markets, which reduces direct memorisation of one simulated path. Each 252-step episode represents one trading year and starts from the constrained-neutral allocation.

Every learned policy receives 60,000 updates with 2,048-step rollouts, minibatches of 512, hidden layers of sizes $[192,128]$, discount $0.99$, GAE parameter $0.95$, and proportional transaction cost of one basis point. Gaussian RCPO actors use three PPO epochs per rollout; Gaussian PPO and Dirichlet actors use four. Environment-interaction budgets are equal, but optimisation budgets and learning-rate schedules are not identical across all controls. Return-best checkpoints are selected using mean excess cumulative return on ten fixed validation continuations. Final deterministic evaluation uses the same 20 disjoint future continuations for every policy; no test branch enters model selection. The continuation seeds, anchor state and transaction-cost convention are shared. The comparison concerns the implemented training schemes, including the objective and regularisation differences in Section~\ref{sec:learning}; one policy seed provides no estimate of training-seed uncertainty.

\subsection{Results}

The archived PPO and RCPO configurations show actor-dependent performance. Relative to PPO, Gaussian RCPO records a lower return, from $4.83\%$ to $4.61\%$, and higher MDD, from $17.99\%$ to $19.14\%$; Dirichlet RCPO records a higher return, from $5.58\%$ to $8.06\%$, and lower MDD, from $17.41\%$ to $16.73\%$. The Dirichlet RCPO control wins in 18 of 20 continuations, although only $40\%$ meet its active drawdown-cost target. The soft allocation penalty produces competitive return but no allocation-feasible test branch. Every CAOSD policy records zero allocation violation, consistent with Proposition~1.

Credit-mode rankings depend on the actor. For Gaussian policies, the counterfactual-cost configuration records higher relative wealth, from $4.61\%$ to $4.97\%$, lower mean MDD, from $19.14\%$ to $18.03\%$, and more drawdown-feasible test branches, from $15\%$ to $30\%$. Turnover is also higher: $7.22\%$ rather than $1.91\%$. Counterfactual reward alone is essentially tied with the Gaussian control, and the joint configuration has lower return and feasibility.

For Dirichlet policies, counterfactual reward attains the highest observed mean return ($8.18\%$) and lowest MDD ($16.52\%$) in the comparison. Its paired return difference from the V2.6 control is only $+0.12$ percentage points (approximate $95\%$ paired $t$-interval $[-0.87,+1.10]$), while win rate decreases from $90\%$ to $85\%$ and turnover rises from $14.27\%$ to $24.00\%$. It is therefore not a conclusive return improvement. The corresponding Gaussian MDD difference has an approximate paired interval of $[-1.90,-0.32]$ percentage points, while its turnover increase is $5.31$ percentage points. These intervals describe variation over 20 continuations conditional on one trained seed; they do not quantify training-seed uncertainty. No counterfactual variant uniformly dominates its matched standalone control.

All eight return-best RCPO policies have positive pooled empirical means of $c-\alpha$ on these continuations. Thus none meets the sample analogue of Eq.~\eqref{eq:adaptive-risk-problem} under deterministic decoding. The feasible-branch rate describes how satisfaction is distributed across test paths; it does not replace this pooled residual statistic or estimate the constraint value of the stochastic training policy.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The results are consistent with the intended division of responsibilities, but support different levels of inference for the two constraint classes. Allocation feasibility follows from the action map under its stated assumptions; drawdown-target satisfaction remains an empirical property of the learned policy and evaluation distribution. No V3.2 return-best policy exceeds a $45\%$ test feasible-branch rate. A separate checkpoint rule that prioritises validation feasible-branch rate before validation return also reaches at most $45\%$ test feasibility; for counterfactual-cost Dirichlet, test relative wealth falls to $0.92\%$. Validation-feasible selection therefore supplies a selection criterion, not an out-of-sample risk guarantee.

The branch-credit comparisons show sensitivity to the training scheme, without establishing a universally preferable construction or isolating the effect of the difference signal. Gaussian and Dirichlet actors respond differently, and combining counterfactual reward and cost does not yield a consistent additive benefit. Additional turnover qualifies the economic interpretation of small improvements in simulated net performance: the reported returns deduct the target-change fee, but do not include richer execution frictions. Evidence is conditional on one policy seed, a finite synthetic test distribution, deterministic evaluation and a deliberately learnable market generator. Repeated selection on fixed validation continuations introduces a further possibility of selection bias. The extent to which these patterns persist across training seeds, historical walk-forward samples and more realistic execution models remains unresolved.

Return, drawdown, turnover and risk-target satisfaction answer distinct questions and should be interpreted jointly. Relative wealth compares terminal outcomes with the same constrained-neutral benchmark; MDD measures the largest peak-to-trough loss; feasible-branch rate tests the episode-average residual-cost target. Similar MDD values need not imply similar feasibility rates, because the training cost depends on the timing, depth and duration of current-drawdown exceedances relative to a changing benchmark threshold. Higher turnover also exposes a policy more strongly to omitted execution costs. Accordingly, a return--risk improvement within this simulator should be interpreted as a conditional performance trade-off rather than evidence of deployment readiness.

\section{Conclusion}

This report develops a modular formulation of sequential constrained allocation and evaluates its implementation through a portfolio instance. CAOSD enforces the specified simplex and two-group exposure requirements through an algebraic action construction, while an RCPO-inspired multiplier adapts the penalty on a benchmark-referenced path cost. The decomposition separates what is guaranteed by action geometry from what must be learned and assessed over trajectories.

Under matched environment-interaction budgets, counterfactual-reward Dirichlet RCPO attains the highest observed mean relative wealth, without a conclusive improvement over its matched control. Joint counterfactual credit offers no consistent advantage, and the pooled path-cost target is not met by the evaluated RCPO policies. The principal finding is therefore methodological: structural action constraints can be enforced independently of stochastic policy optimisation, but neither that feasibility construction nor adaptive average-cost learning establishes reliable trajectory-risk control. The empirical conclusions remain specific to the reported synthetic-market study.

\section{Feasibility of the Two-Group CAOSD Map}
\label{app:feasibility}

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition 1.}
For completeness, we verify the stated feasibility property of the CAOSD construction \cite{winkel2023}. Since $\ell_1,\ell_2\in[0,1]$,
$0\leq z_1\leq\min\{\ell_1,\ell_2\}$ and $z_2=\ell_1-z_1\geq0$.
A non-empty second branch gives $q_2\in[0,1]$; an empty branch gives $q_2=0$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  0\leq z_3
     \leq\ell_2-z_1
     \leq1-\ell_1,\qquad
  z_4=1-\ell_1-z_3\geq0.
  \label{eq:proof-mass-bound}
\end{equation}
The upper bound uses
$\ell_2-z_1=\min\{\ell_2,1-\ell_1\}$.
The coefficients sum to one by definition.

It remains to check that an empty branch cannot remove positive mass from the allocation. Non-emptiness of the feasible set excludes $V_k=\varnothing$ whenever $\ell_k>0$. Thus $K_2=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_1=z_1=z_2=0$, while $K_3=\varnothing$ implies $\ell_2=z_1=z_3=0$. If $K_1=\varnothing$, the groups are disjoint; feasibility then requires $\ell_1+\ell_2\leq1$, giving $z_1=0$. Since $K_4=\mathcal E$ is non-empty, every positive coefficient multiplies a non-negative unit-mass branch. Branches with zero coefficients contribute nothing, regardless of whether their supports are empty. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf T(\bm u)\geq\bm0,\qquad
  \mathbf1^\top\mathsf T(\bm u)=\sum_{i=1}^{4}z_i=1.
  \label{eq:proof-simplex}
\end{equation}
The prescribed supports further imply
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j\in V_1}a_j\geq z_1+z_2=\ell_1,\qquad
  \sum_{j\in V_2}a_j\geq z_1+z_2q_2+z_3\geq\ell_2.
  \label{eq:proof-exposure}
\end{equation}
All omitted contributions to either group are non-negative. This proves simplex membership and both exposure bounds, including overlapping groups, disjoint groups and zero-mass branches. \hfill$\square$

In floating-point arithmetic, the implementation clips negligible negative round-off and normalises the final allocation. Numerical feasibility diagnostics are interpreted relative to their tolerance.

\section{Portfolio Parameters and Numerical Conventions}
\label{app:instance}

\subsection{Instance parameters}

With the simulator's zero-based entity identifiers, the experimental groups and minimum exposures are
\begin{equation}
  V_1=\{1,4,6\},\quad \ell_1=0.50;\qquad
  V_2=\{2,4,7\},\quad \ell_2=0.40.
  \label{eq:portfolio-groups}
\end{equation}
These identifiers describe the instance without fixing $N$ in the general formulation. Since $\ell_1+\ell_2<1$, the first coefficient is identically zero and branch~1 receives no policy update. Branches~2--4 remain trainable; the overlap still influences $q_2$ and $z_3$. Agent and benchmark start at the CAOSD image of uniform branch weights. The benchmark retains this target and incurs neither an initial target-change fee nor subsequent target-change fees under the simulator's accounting convention.

The parameters in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth}--\eqref{eq:portfolio-alpha} and \eqref{eq:lambda} are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
    \kappa=10^{-4},\qquad b_{\min}=0.05,\qquad
    \beta=0.90,\qquad s_C=0.10,\qquad \nu=0.05,\\
    \varepsilon_R=10^{-6},\qquad \varepsilon_W=10^{-12},\qquad
    \eta_\uparrow=0.00075,\qquad\eta_\downarrow=0.01,\qquad\lambda_0=0.
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:instance-parameters}
\end{equation}
The benchmark adapts the drawdown threshold and hence the cost allowance. The multiplier responds once per rollout to the realised mean residual; neither mechanism projects the portfolio onto a path-risk-feasible set. Current drawdown determines the training cost, while maximum drawdown is retained as a diagnostic and evaluation statistic.

\subsection{Accounting and numerical safeguards}

For two probability vectors,
$T_t\leq\lVert\bm w_t\rVert_1+\lVert\bm w_{t-1}\rVert_1=2$.
Moreover, the portfolio's gross return is a convex combination of entity gross returns. Hence
\begin{equation}
  g_{t+1}\geq
     \min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})-2\kappa.
  \label{eq:positive-growth-bound}
\end{equation}
The condition $\min_{i\in\mathcal E}(1+R_{t+1,i})>2\kappa$ is sufficient for positive net growth. It is not implied by simplex membership, and the Gaussian return model does not guarantee it. Numerical floors keep individual updates defined, but do not model liquidation, default or an economically self-financing recovery. If either floor is active, reward need not equal the log wealth increment. In exact arithmetic, positive floored growth and $W_0=W_0^B=1$ keep both running peaks at least one and drawdown denominators non-zero; this does not rule out floating-point underflow over arbitrary horizons.

Exact holdings-level self-financing would require measuring trades from return-drifted pre-trade holdings and accounting for fee payment when determining post-trade wealth and weights \cite{jiang2017}. Equation~\eqref{eq:portfolio-net-growth} instead deducts a proportional target-change proxy. Full-vector turnover includes reserve transfers, but slippage, tiered fees and market impact are absent. A constant-target benchmark is also not charged for rebalancing caused by holding drift. The empirical results must be read under these accounting assumptions.

For numerical PPO evaluation, joint and conditional log-probability differences are clipped to $[-20,20]$ before exponentiation. Equation~\eqref{eq:ppo-ratios} gives the unmodified ratios, recovered when this safeguard is inactive. Advantage normalisation uses the rollout mean and population standard deviation with an additive $10^{-8}$ denominator stabiliser. Actual, benchmark and auxiliary path memories persist across rollout boundaries and reset at episode boundaries.

\subsection{Policy independence of the benchmark allowance}

Let $\mathbb P_x$ denote the law of the exogenous market trajectory. Market sampling, horizon $H$, benchmark target and initial benchmark state are fixed independently of $\theta$. Its allowance is therefore a functional $\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})$ of that trajectory. For every admissible allocation policy,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]
      &=\mathbb E_{\mathbb P_x}\!\left[
        \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}(x_{0:t+1})\right],\\
    \nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta^{\mathrm h}}\!\left[
      \frac1H\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\alpha_{t+1}\right]&=\bm0.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:allowance-independence}
\end{equation}
The expectation is constant in $\theta$, so no interchange of differentiation and integration is needed. Fixed discount weights give the same policy-independence property. This explains why the global critic can omit the allowance from its policy-dependent cost target. It does not remove the actor--dual discount mismatch or make the branch objectives exact Lagrangian gradients.
